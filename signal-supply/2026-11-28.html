The plain version

Before a sliver of silicon becomes a working AI chip, it gets bathed, over and over, in extremely strong, extremely pure acid. Two of the workhorses are sulfuric acid, which strips away leftover photographic "resist" and grime between manufacturing steps, and hydrofluoric acid, which etches away oxide layers with surgical precision. Neither acid is exotic on its own — your car battery has sulfuric acid in it — but the grade a chip fab needs is so pure that a handful of stray metal atoms per billion can ruin a wafer. Making that grade, and shipping it safely, is its own small, concentrated industry.

In 2026, that industry got hit from two directions at once. A military conflict around the Strait of Hormuz, a sea lane nowhere near a single chip fab, choked off a big share of the world's sulfur, the raw ingredient sulfuric acid is made from; the price of sulfuric acid landing on the U.S. Gulf Coast rose from about $155 a metric ton in February to roughly $400 by early May. China separately banned sulfuric acid exports for several months, squeezing supply further. At the same time, a different squeeze was building: South Korea's chipmakers, through the specialty chemical companies that refine industrial acid into chip-grade hydrofluoric acid, get the large majority of their raw material from China — and in May, they had to buy a lot of it at a roughly 40% markup.

None of this stopped chip production. It did add real, if usually invisible, cost to every wafer that went through a Samsung or SK hynix fab this year. South Korea's answer — a new domestic hydrofluoric acid plant in Ulsan — is scheduled to start mass production this quarter: a small, boring-sounding fix for a chokepoint most people never think about.

The expert version

Advanced-node fabrication is chemically intensive: a leading-edge logic wafer can pass through hundreds of wet-cleaning and etch steps, many using sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄, often as "piranha" mixtures with hydrogen peroxide) to strip photoresist and organic residue, and hydrofluoric acid (HF) to selectively etch silicon dioxide and native oxide layers. Both must be electronic-grade, with metallic-contamination limits down to parts-per-trillion, which restricts production to a small number of qualified specialty-chemical refiners — among them Formosa Plastics in Taiwan and several Korean and Japanese refiners that import feedstock and purify it to spec.

2026 exposed two independent fragilities in that supply chain. First, a military conflict centered on the Strait of Hormuz disrupted Gulf sulfur and sulfuric-acid trade: S&P Global Platts tracked CFR US Gulf sulfuric acid rising from about $155/mt (Feb. 25) to roughly $400/mt (May 6) — a roughly 158% increase — while benchmark sulfur (FOB US Gulf) moved from about $650/mt at the war's early-April start past $1,000/mt by midyear. China's own April–August 2026 export ban on sulfuric acid compounded the shortage before a mid-June US-Iran ceasefire began easing it, though physical supply stayed tight well after prices peaked. Second, and more structurally, South Korean specialty-chemical refiners depend on China for the large majority of the raw anhydrous hydrofluoric acid (AHF) they import and purify into semiconductor-grade HF — Korean trade press puts the figure near 90% for some refiners, a legacy of Japan's 2019 export restrictions on Korea, which pushed Korean buyers toward Chinese AHF rather than rebuilding domestic capacity. TrendForce reports Korean buyers made large AHF purchases from China in May 2026 at prices roughly 40% above prior levels.

The fixes are capital-intensive and slow: Formosa Plastics raised Q3 2026 electronic-grade HF prices while its own capacity expansion isn't due until 2027, and BGF EcoMaterials' Fluorine Korea subsidiary is bringing a 50,000-ton/year AHF plant in Ulsan to mass production this quarter — the first real dent in Korea's China dependency for this specific input, arriving roughly six years after the vulnerability became visible.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: Chip chokepoints aren't only rare earths and lithography machines — they're also tanker-loads of acid, and 2026 showed both a war-driven shortage and a single-country dependency hitting the same unglamorous chemicals at once.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: Electronic-grade wet-chemical supply is a legible, underwatched input-cost and geopolitical-exposure signal for memory and logic fabs, with direct read-through to Samsung/SK hynix margins and Taiwan's chemical-refining capacity.
