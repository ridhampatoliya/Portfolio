October 9, 2026 MARITIME INSURANCE

A Tanker's Insurance Bill for One Trip Through Hormuz Went From $375,000 to $15 Million. The Cargo Didn't Get More Dangerous — the Pricing Did.

Since the US-Israeli war on Iran spilled into the Strait of Hormuz in late February 2026, a quarter of the world's seaborne oil and a fifth of its LNG have been moving through a waterway that regular marine insurance no longer covers. The gap is filled by "war risk" insurance — a separate policy, repriced roughly every seven days, that behaves less like a fixed cost and more like a live danger meter. At its peak this year, insurers were charging up to 10% of a ship's hull value for a single transit. That meter, not a shortage of oil or gas itself, is what's been quietly repricing energy for everyone downstream — including the chip fabs and data centers that run on it.

Key takeaway War risk cover is quoted on roughly 7-day cycles, not annual ones, so a bad week in the news — a struck tanker, a mine report, a stalled talk — can add millions to a single ship's bill without a single barrel of oil or cubic meter of gas changing hands. Reported rates for Hormuz transits swung from a pre-war baseline near 0.1–0.25% of hull value to a peak of roughly 7.5–10% in July, per Marsh and Insurance Business, with Xinhua describing around 5% as an emerging norm later in the year. Sources disagree on the exact current figure, so treat any single number as an estimate within that range, not a settled price.

The plain version

Picture buying car insurance, except instead of one annual premium, your insurer re-quotes you every single week based on how dangerous your commute looked that week. That's roughly what has happened to oil and gas tankers in the Strait of Hormuz this year.

The strait is a narrow passage between Iran and Oman that carries roughly a quarter of the world's seaborne oil and about a fifth of its liquefied natural gas (LNG). When a US-Israeli war on Iran broke out in late February 2026 and Iran began boarding ships, laying mines, and firing on tankers, ordinary marine insurance — which has excluded "acts of war" since roughly World War I — stopped covering transits through the strait. To even sail, shipowners now have to buy a second, separate policy: war-risk insurance, priced not once a year but roughly once a week, by underwriters who track the news as closely as any trader.

Before the war, that weekly add-on cost next to nothing — a small fraction of one percent of a ship's value. At the worst points this year, reported rates spiked to roughly 10%. For a $150 million tanker, that's the difference between a $375,000 line item and a $15 million one, for the exact same ship carrying the exact same cargo on the exact same route.

That bill doesn't stay with the shipowner — it gets folded into the price of the oil and gas on board. Which is how a crisis measured in missile strikes and mined water ends up, a few steps downstream, as a slightly higher power bill for a chip fab or an AI data center on the other side of the world.

The expert version

Standard marine hull & machinery (H&M) and protection & indemnity (P&I) policies have carried a war exclusion clause since the aftermath of the First World War, when underwriters classified state-on-state conflict as an uninsurable, highly correlated risk rather than an ordinary marine peril. Coverage for that excluded risk is sold separately, as war risk insurance, typically underwritten on renewable terms as short as seven days rather than a standard annual policy — a structure that lets the market reprice continuously as a conflict evolves instead of committing to a year-long rate up front.

Pricing is anchored to the Lloyd's Market Association's Joint War Committee (JWC), whose "Listed Areas" flag higher-risk zones; being added to, or remaining on, that list is effectively what authorizes insurers to demand the extra premium. Reported rates for Hormuz transits in 2026 moved sharply and, per multiple outlets, inconsistently: from a pre-war baseline near 0.1–0.25% of a vessel's hull value, to a reported 3–10% at acute points — Marsh cited 7.5–10% in late July, Xinhua described roughly 5% as an emerging norm, and Insurance Business reported 3–8% still prevailing in late August. On a $150 million tanker, a 10% rate produces a $15 million premium for a single transit, against roughly $375,000 at a 0.25% pre-war rate.

A second constraint compounds price: Marsh estimates total global marine war-risk hull capacity at only $2.5–3 billion, so when many high-value vessels seek cover at once, capacity itself — not just incident frequency — becomes a pricing input. That premium passes through into freight and charter rates and from there into landed fuel costs: Hormuz carries an estimated 20–25% of global seaborne oil and roughly 20% of LNG, and Qatar's Ras Laffan complex (about a fifth of world LNG exports) briefly shut under force majeure in March 2026, pushing Asian spot LNG prices above $20/mmBtu — a direct input cost for the power-hungry fabs and data centers on the receiving end, independent of any disruption to chips or hardware themselves.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: insurers can reprice a chokepoint's danger week to week, long before — or even without — any actual shortage hitting the dock, and that repricing shows up as a real cost for anyone running power-hungry fabs or data centers downstream.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: war-risk premiums and the JWC Listed Areas framework are a leading, fast-moving indicator of chokepoint cost — one that reprices energy inputs for fabs and data centers on a weekly cycle, well ahead of the slower-moving physical-disruption data most capacity models rely on.
