November 23, 2026 BATTERY RECYCLING

America Makes an Estimated 60,000 Tons of Battery-Recycling "Black Mass" a Year. It Just Banned Exporting It — and Can Refine Well Under Half of That at Home.

Shred a dead EV or grid battery and you get a dark, metal-rich powder called black mass — the raw material for the next battery, if someone can refine it. In August, Washington banned shipping that powder overseas to keep its lithium, cobalt and nickel onshore. The catch: American refiners were never built to process anywhere near all of it.

Key takeaway This isn't a story about running out of critical minerals — the US arguably has too much black mass, not too little. The ban removed the export market that used to clear the surplus. Until domestic refining capacity catches up, which could take years, the material has nowhere obvious to go.

Six months into the ban, roughly 30,000 tons of black mass have been produced domestically. Refiners have processed less than half of it — the rest sits in a growing stockpile with no export outlet.

The plain version

When an EV or grid battery dies, recyclers don't throw it out — they shred it into a dark, dense powder called black mass, packed with the same lithium, cobalt, nickel and graphite that went into the battery in the first place. Turning that powder back into battery-grade material takes a specialized refinery, and the US simply hasn't built very many. For years, the practical answer was to ship surplus black mass overseas — mostly to refiners in China and South Korea — who had the furnaces and chemical plants to finish the job.

That pipeline just got cut. In August 2026, the Trump administration invoked the Defense Production Act and had the Commerce Department ban exports of black mass (and separately, tungsten scrap) for one year, through August 2027, with case-by-case exceptions. The goal is straightforward: stop critical minerals from leaving the country, and starve a Chinese refining industry the US has spent years trying to compete with.

The problem is capacity, not intent. By one widely cited estimate, the US produces roughly 60,000 tons of black mass a year but can only refine around 25,000 tons of it domestically — other analysts think the real gap is even wider. That mismatch lands on a sector already bruised: two of the most prominent US battery recyclers, Li-Cycle and Ascend Elements, both went bankrupt within the past year, and their plants have since changed hands. A ban meant to lock critical minerals inside US borders could, in the short run, mostly just strand them in a warehouse.

The expert version

Black mass is the intermediate product of mechanically shredding end-of-life lithium-ion cells and battery-manufacturing scrap: a dark powder reported to make up roughly 40–50% of a cell's total weight, containing recoverable lithium (sample estimates run roughly 2–6% by weight), cobalt (5–20%), nickel (5–15%), manganese, copper, aluminum, and graphite from the anode (reported as high as ~40% by weight in at least one industrial sample). Composition varies substantially by cell chemistry and feedstock, and there's no single standardized assay. Shredding (pre-processing) is far less capital-intensive than the hydrometallurgical or pyrometallurgical refining that converts black mass into battery-grade lithium, nickel and cobalt salts — and the US has built out considerably more of the former than the latter, which is the structural root of this policy's bind.

The restriction moved through two steps: Presidential Determination No. 2026-19, signed July 30, 2026 under the Defense Production Act, authorized the Commerce Department to restrict exports of critical-mineral-bearing waste and scrap. Commerce's Bureau of Industry and Security then issued an implementing Temporary Final Rule on August 6, requiring an export license for black mass from lithium-ion battery recycling (and tungsten waste/scrap) effective August 27, 2026 through August 27, 2027, with case-by-case exceptions — including for black mass sent abroad for refining if the refined output is returned to the US.

Domestic capacity estimates diverge sharply because no comprehensive public inventory exists. S&P Global Commodity Insights estimates roughly 60,000 metric tons of US black mass production a year against about 25,000 metric tons of domestic refining capacity. The Union of Concerned Scientists estimates a wider gap — around 7,000 metric tons of refining capacity against 65,000 metric tons of feedstock — and projects capacity won't close that gap until 2030. Fastmarkets separately estimates US shredding (pre-processing) capacity near 94,000 metric tons a year, with utilization below 50% at some facilities due to tight scrap supply, a sign the bottleneck sits downstream in refining, not in collection. Before the ban, Asian refiners bought the majority of surplus US black mass; without that outlet, analysts expect falling domestic black mass prices and further financial strain on a sector already weakened by the 2025–2026 bankruptcies of Li-Cycle (US assets since acquired by Glencore) and Ascend Elements (whose Covington, Georgia lithium-carbonate facility was taken over by R3 Lithium).

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: A policy meant to keep critical minerals in America could instead strand them — recyclers feeding the next generation of EV and grid batteries just lost their main buyer, and domestic refiners aren't built out yet to replace it.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: The ban bets that restricting exports forces refining capacity to materialize faster than market signals alone would — but Defense Production Act authority doesn't build plants, and a capacity gap estimated at anywhere from 2x to 9x means the policy could strand recyclable material for years before it secures it.
