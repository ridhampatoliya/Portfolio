November 17, 2026 FIBER OPTICS

China's One-Year Truce on an Obscure Metal Expires in 10 Days. It's the Dopant That Lets Light Bend Through Every Strand of AI Fiber.

On November 9, 2025, China's Ministry of Commerce suspended its ban on exporting germanium — along with gallium and antimony — to the United States, part of the trade truce struck that fall between Presidents Trump and Xi. The suspension runs out November 27, 2026, ten days after this edition lands, and Beijing hasn't publicly signaled whether it will renew it. Germanium is an obscure, brittle metal almost nobody outside a chemistry class has heard of. It also happens to be the ingredient doped into the glass core of nearly every strand of fiber-optic cable — including the optical links now wiring AI GPU clusters together inside data centers.

Key takeaway Optical fiber only works because its glass core is doped with germanium dioxide (GeO₂) to bend light just enough that it bounces down the middle of the strand instead of leaking out the sides — a property called total internal reflection. China supplies roughly two-thirds of the world's germanium, almost entirely as a byproduct of zinc smelting, so output can't simply ramp up when a price spike hits. Beijing banned exporting it to the US in December 2024, suspended that ban for a year in November 2025, and that suspension lapses November 27 — ten days from today. Licensing requirements and a ban on sales to military end-users stay in force no matter what happens next.

With no germanium, the core and cladding are the same glass — there's no refractive-index boundary to reflect off. Light just crosses straight through, the way it would through a plain window. This is why “fiber” without a dopant isn't really a waveguide at all.

The plain version

Every AI data center is stitched together with thin strands of glass — fiber-optic cable — carrying pulses of light between racks of GPUs instead of electricity through copper. For that light to travel any real distance without leaking out the sides, the glass core has to be laced with a pinch of an obscure, brittle metal called germanium. Mixed into the core as the fiber is made, germanium bends light just enough that it keeps bouncing down the dead center of the strand — a trick called total internal reflection — instead of escaping through the walls. Without it, "fiber" is just a very thin, very expensive window.

China supplies roughly two-thirds of the world's germanium, and it doesn't even mine it directly — germanium is a leftover captured while smelting zinc ore, so its supply rises and falls with the zinc business, not with anyone's actual demand for germanium. In December 2024, Beijing added germanium, along with gallium and antimony, to its export-control list and banned shipping it to the US outright, retaliation for American chip-equipment restrictions. A year ago, as part of the trade truce between Presidents Trump and Xi, China hit pause on that specific ban — but didn't cancel it. That pause runs out November 27, ten days from today. Even while it's paused, US buyers still need Beijing's permission for every shipment, and sales to anyone flagged as a military buyer stay blocked no matter what.

Meanwhile, AI's hunger for fiber keeps growing: modern GPU clusters are wired rack-to-rack with optical links so thousands of chips can act like one giant computer, and germanium prices have already more than doubled since 2024 as the AI buildout competes with everyone else for a metal that was never easy to scale up in the first place.

The expert version

Optical fiber guides light down a glass core via total internal reflection (TIR), which requires the core's refractive index (n₁) to exceed the surrounding cladding's (n₂). Pure fused silica alone doesn't provide enough contrast for a practical waveguide, so manufacturers dope the core with germanium dioxide (GeO₂) during preform fabrication — typically via modified chemical vapor deposition (MCVD) or outside vapor deposition (OVD) — raising n₁ by an amount tied to dopant concentration. GeO₂ is the industry-standard core dopant because it integrates into the silica lattice without materially increasing attenuation, and its refractive-index contribution scales predictably with concentration, which is what lets manufacturers engineer a fiber's numerical aperture and mode structure to spec.

Germanium itself isn't mined as a primary ore. It's recovered almost entirely as a byproduct — mostly from sphalerite (zinc sulfide) smelting residues, with a smaller share from coal fly ash — so its output is hostage to decisions made in the zinc and coal industries, not to germanium demand. That structural inelasticity is a large part of why a price spike can't simply be answered with more supply. China's refining dominance, commonly put at around two-thirds of global output (a precise, current country-level USGS breakdown isn't itemized), traces directly to its large zinc-smelting base.

On the trade side: China's Ministry of Commerce added germanium, gallium, and antimony to its dual-use export control list in December 2024 (Announcement No. 46), banning outright exports to the US. MOFCOM Announcement No. 72, issued November 9, 2025 as part of the broader Trump-Xi de-escalation, suspended that US-specific ban until November 27, 2026 — but left the underlying licensing regime and a military-end-user prohibition in force. US buyers can still obtain germanium, but only with case-by-case sign-off from Beijing, and the suspension itself is a unilateral, revocable grant rather than a negotiated, binding commitment.

Price action backs up the supply strain: USGS put germanium metal's annual average European price at $4,100/kg in 2025, roughly double 2024's $1,991/kg, and trade trackers reported China-linked benchmarks pushing toward record territory through 2026. Corning — the largest Western fiber maker — has cited AI-driven demand explicitly in new capacity announcements, adding a second, independent source of upward pressure on top of the supply-side fragility.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: A metal most people have never heard of sits inside the literal wiring of the AI boom, and the country that supplies most of it has a decision about selling it to the US coming up in 10 days.
