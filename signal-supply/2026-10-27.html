October 27, 2026 AIR FREIGHT

Moving One EUV Chipmaking Machine Can Take Three Boeing 747 Cargo Planes. The World Is Running Short on Planes Like That.

Some machines are too big, too fragile, or too urgent to put on a container ship. For decades the chip industry's answer was simple: fly it. That answer is getting harder — and even the maker of chipmaking's most important machine is starting to look for a boat instead.

Key takeaway Intel says delivering a single EUV lithography system to its Oregon fab takes three Boeing 747 freighters, 40 specialized containers, and 20 trucks. The dedicated wide-body freighter fleet that kind of shipment depends on is stretched thin industry-wide: airlines are sitting on a backlog of more than 17,000 unfilled wide-body aircraft orders that would take over a decade to clear at current build rates, while Red Sea rerouting alone has pulled an estimated 12% of global air-cargo capacity out of circulation.

The plain version

Think about what it actually takes to move an EUV machine — the only tool on Earth capable of printing the smallest circuits inside an AI chip. Intel says delivering just one to its Oregon fab takes three Boeing 747 cargo jets, 40 shipping containers, and 20 trucks. The machine itself weighs close to 200 tons and is built from roughly 100,000 parts.

That kind of shipment depends on something most people never think about: a global fleet of dedicated cargo planes — mostly aging 747s and 777s — that haul the stuff too big, too fragile, or too time-sensitive to wait weeks on a container ship. That fleet is stretched thin right now. Airlines have placed orders for more than 17,000 new wide-body jets, but at current production rates it would take over a decade just to clear that backlog: Boeing is building roughly two wide-bodies a month against an internal target near five; Airbus about five against a target of eleven. Meanwhile, ships and planes alike keep rerouting around the Red Sea's Houthi threat, which alone has pulled an estimated 12% of the world's air-cargo capacity out of circulation — even as demand for air freight keeps climbing faster than the supply of planes to carry it.

The company that makes those EUV machines, ASML, is already adjusting. In 2024 it ran its first trial shipping part of an EUV system by sea instead of air, working toward a stated goal of moving its chipmaking platform onto specially designed sea containers by 2030. For equipment this heavy and this valuable, flying it has always been the easy, expensive default. Now there's barely enough flying capacity left to make that default work.

The expert version

The binding constraint is the dedicated freighter fleet — purpose-built cargo aircraft (747-8F, 777F, MD-11F) and passenger-to-freighter (P2F) conversions — not belly capacity on passenger jets, which can't physically accommodate oversize, high-value shipments like lithography subsystems. Industry estimates put the current order backlog for new wide-body aircraft, passenger and freighter combined, above 17,000 units; at present manufacturing rates that backlog would take more than a decade to clear. Boeing is producing roughly two wide-bodies a month against an internal target near five, including its delayed 777-8 freighter variant; Airbus is building about five A350s a month against a 2027 target of eleven. P2F conversion supply, historically a release valve during freighter shortages, is shrinking too, as fewer early-generation wide-bodies remain available to convert.

Demand-side pressure compounds the shortage. Houthi attacks in the Red Sea have forced persistent route diversions, removing an estimated 12% of global air-cargo capacity from circulation even as freight volumes grew roughly 4% year-over-year in the first half of 2026 against only about 1% capacity growth — a widening gap industry analysts expect to persist through the end of the decade.

For chip-equipment logistics specifically, the stakes are unusually literal. Intel describes a single EUV lithography system — a nearly 200-ton, ~100,000-part machine — as requiring three 747 freighter loads, 40 specialized containers, and 20 trucks to deliver to one fab. ASML's own 2025 reporting shows air transport accounts for roughly 95% of its logistics carbon footprint (about 306 of 322 kt CO2e), and in 2024 the company ran its first sea-freight trial of a reticle-stage subsystem with APM Terminals — part of a stated multi-year plan to shift its EUV platform onto specially designed sea containers by 2030. That timeline is itself a bet that scarce, expensive freighter capacity will only get harder to secure before it gets easier.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: The AI boom depends on machines too big, fragile, and valuable to ship any way but by air — and the planes built for that job are running out faster than new ones are arriving.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: Freighter capacity has quietly become a constraint on the chip-equipment supply chain alongside fabs, export controls, and critical minerals — and even the industry's most important supplier is re-engineering its own hardware to need it less.
