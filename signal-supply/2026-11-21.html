November 21, 2026 AI CHIP EXPORTS

Nvidia Booked $2.4 Billion Selling AI Chips to China Last Quarter. This Quarter It's Shipping Zero — Even Though Washington Still Says Yes.

Three parties each hold a veto over whether an export-approved Nvidia chip reaches a Chinese AI lab: Washington, Beijing, and Nvidia itself. This month, two of the three are quietly using it — and none of them will say exactly why.

Key takeaway An export control is never one switch — it's a chain of separate, discretionary checkpoints, and the whole shipment stops if any single link in that chain goes quiet. Right now the direct-import route to China is jammed by Beijing's own customs discretion, not Washington's, while a brand-new U.S. rule just closed a completely different route (renting the same compute remotely through a data center in a third country) from the other end.

The plain version

Picture buying a car that needs three separate green lights before it can leave the lot: the manufacturer has to agree to sell it, your own country has to let it cross the border, and — this is the strange part — the dealership can still refuse to hand over the keys at the very last second, even after everyone else already said yes. That's roughly the situation right now with one of the most important products in the world economy: Nvidia's H200, an AI chip built to train and run the models behind today's AI tools.

Last quarter, Nvidia actually sold $2.4 billion worth of these chips to Chinese buyers — real money, real hardware, shipped out the door, under a license the U.S. government had already approved. This quarter, on a November 17 earnings call, the company's finance chief (now also its interim CEO, after Jensen Huang abruptly resigned on November 11 following a personal scandal) told investors Nvidia expects to ship close to none. She didn't explain why.

Here's the twist: Washington's approval for the H200 hasn't changed. The holdup looks like it's happening on China's side instead. Reports put roughly 600,000 of these chips sitting, boxed, in warehouses in Hong Kong — a free port that's legally outside mainland China's customs border — waiting for a Beijing clearance that simply hasn't arrived. China spent years publicly pushing the U.S. to unblock these chips. Now that Washington has partly relented, it looks like Beijing itself is the one slow-walking the paperwork, possibly to protect its own chipmakers or to hold leverage in a wider trade standoff.

The expert version

On Nvidia's fiscal Q3 FY2027 earnings call (November 17, 2026), CFO Colette Kress — now also interim CEO following Jensen Huang's November 11 resignation — disclosed $2.4 billion in licensed Hopper-architecture data-center revenue from China for the quarter ended October 25. Those sales moved under the existing Commerce Department export license covering the H200, a carve-out that survives even though Nvidia's newer Blackwell and Rubin architectures remain fully embargoed from China under separate Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) end-use controls. Kress told analysts that Q4 guidance assumes effectively no China data-center compute revenue — not because the license changed, but because Nvidia itself has stopped shipping, for reasons it hasn't disclosed.

The likelier explanation sits on the import side, not the export side. Multiple outlets, citing Reuters, report that roughly 600,000 H200 units are held in bonded warehouses in Hong Kong — a free port outside mainland customs territory — after Chinese authorities declined to clear large-volume imports onto the mainland. Beijing hasn't published a formal ban; the pattern looks more like discretionary slow-walking, consistent with 2026 guidance steering state-linked buyers toward domestic accelerators (Huawei's Ascend line, Cambricon) even where raw performance still trails Nvidia's silicon.

A separate front closed the same week. BIS issued an interim final rule, "Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors," effective November 19, 2026, targeting a workaround where a Chinese entity skips importing hardware and instead rents equivalent compute remotely from a data center outside China. The rule adds license requirements on data centers in Macau and in a Commerce-designated jurisdiction list that multiple law-firm trackers say explicitly names Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia, Thailand, and Brazil, once a facility's compute passes an aggregate threshold of 12 million "Total Processing Performance" units — a BIS metric sizing chip compute capacity across one site or several "linked" data centers. It also rescinds the prior AI Diffusion Rule's tiered-country framework and gives data centers already above threshold a 90-day window to seek a license.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: A chip doesn't need an enemy to stop moving — it just needs any one of three gatekeepers, buyer's government, seller's government, or the seller itself, to go quiet. Right now two of three have.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: Two chokepoints closed from opposite directions in the same ten days: Beijing's customs discretion is jamming the direct-import route, while Washington's new rule just licensed the remote-access bypass. Export control, in practice, is a chain of discretionary checkpoints, not a single switch — and this week both ends of the chain moved at once.
