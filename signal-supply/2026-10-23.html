October 23, 2026 URBAN MINING

A Dead Hard Drive Is a Richer Rare-Earth Mine Than Most Ore. Microsoft Just Recovered 90% of the Metal From 50,000 Pounds of Them.

Somewhere inside every spinning hard drive is a small, strong permanent magnet made of neodymium, praseodymium and dysprosium — the same family of metals China dominates and has made steadily harder to buy. Starting last year, Microsoft began treating its own retired data-center hard drives as an ore body instead of landfill. The pilot processed about 50,000 pounds of them and recovered roughly 90% of the metal inside.

Key takeaway In April 2025, Microsoft partnered with Western Digital and two specialty recyclers, Critical Materials Recycling and PedalPoint Recycling, on a pilot using an acid-free dissolution process — not conventional smelting — to pull neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium, gold and copper out of roughly 50,000 pounds of shredded, end-of-life data-center hard drives and caddies. The partners report a 90% recovery yield and claim a 95% lower carbon footprint than mining and refining the same metals from ore; those are company-disclosed figures, and Microsoft has not publicly said how many pounds of rare-earth metal the pilot actually yielded. By February 2026, Microsoft said it would extend the approach to new recycling sites in Texas, Wales and Australia. The strategic backdrop: the U.S. Geological Survey puts China's share of global rare-earth refining capacity at roughly 90%, and Beijing's export-licensing rules now reach magnets built anywhere in the world if they contain even trace Chinese-origin material.

The plain version

Inside every hard drive spinning away in a data center is a tiny but surprisingly powerful magnet, built from rare-earth metals most people have never heard of: neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium. These are the magnets that snap a drive's read/write arm into position thousands of times a second. They're also the same family of metals used in EV motors, wind turbines and fighter-jet components — and China mines and refines the overwhelming majority of the world's supply, with export rules that have gotten tighter and harder to predict.

Here's the twist: once a hard drive gets old and gets pulled from service, that magnet doesn't disappear. It's sitting in a pile of shredded e-waste somewhere. Mining and recycling engineers have pointed out for years that a fistful of scrapped electronics can hold a far richer concentration of rare-earth metal than a fistful of dirt dug out of an actual mine. The problem has always been getting it back out economically, without a smelter and a mountain of acid.

Last year, Microsoft decided to try anyway. Working with hard-drive maker Western Digital and two specialty recyclers, it ran about 50,000 pounds of shredded, retired data-center hard drives through a chemical process designed to dissolve out the valuable metals selectively, rather than melting everything down in a furnace. The companies say they recovered roughly 90% of the rare-earth and precious metal that went in — neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium, plus gold and copper — and claim doing it this way produces about 95% less carbon than digging the same metal out of the ground.

Microsoft has since said it's expanding the idea to new recycling sites in Texas, Wales and Australia. It's a rounding error next to global rare-earth demand today. But every AI data center on Earth is quietly generating more of exactly this kind of scrap, every single year, for the rest of the decade — and for the first time, someone is treating it like a mine instead of a dumpster.

The expert version

The economics here rest on ore grade. A sintered neodymium-iron-boron (NdFeB) magnet — the type used in a hard drive's voice-coil actuator — runs roughly 25–32% rare-earth content by mass, chiefly neodymium and praseodymium, with smaller dysprosium and terbium additions for thermal stability at operating temperature. Compare that to mined ore: Mountain Pass, California's bastnäsite deposit, one of the richest still in commercial production, runs around 8% rare-earth oxide (REO); most operating deposits worldwide run closer to 1–3%. By concentration, a separated magnet is a higher-grade "deposit" than nearly anything still in the ground. The limiting factor has long been separation chemistry and collection logistics — sorting magnet-bearing scrap out of mixed waste streams and dissolving it cleanly — not scarcity of the metal itself.

In April 2025, Microsoft, Western Digital, and recyclers Critical Materials Recycling and PedalPoint Recycling launched a pilot applying an acid-free dissolution process — which selectively dissolves target metals from shredded feedstock rather than relying on high-temperature smelting or strong-acid leaching, the conventional e-waste recycling routes — to roughly 50,000 pounds (about 22.7 metric tons) of shredded, end-of-life data-center hard drives, mounting caddies and related hardware. The partners report recovering neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium, gold and copper at a combined ~90% yield, with a life-cycle assessment claiming a 95% reduction in greenhouse-gas emissions versus primary mining and refining. Those figures are company-disclosed and not independently audited; one outlet reported that a Microsoft executive declined to disclose the absolute tonnage of rare-earth oxide actually recovered, so treat the percentages as self-reported pending third-party verification.

By February 2026, Microsoft said it would extend its Circular Centers model — a broader hardware reuse-and-recycling network it separately reports runs at a 90.9% overall component reuse/recycle rate — to new sites in San Antonio, Texas; Cardiff, Wales; and New South Wales, Australia. The strategic context: USGS estimates put China's share of global rare-earth separation and refining capacity at roughly 90%, and recent Chinese export-licensing rules extend jurisdiction to foreign-made magnets containing as little as 0.1% Chinese-origin rare-earth material by value, wherever they were manufactured — which makes a verifiably non-Chinese, recycled feedstock a genuine supply-chain hedge rather than purely an ESG exercise.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: As China tightens its grip on rare-earth refining, the AI boom's own retired hardware is turning into one of the only rare-earth supply chains that doesn't run through Beijing.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: A recycled, magnet-grade feedstock sidesteps both mined-ore scarcity and Chinese export licensing at once — which is why hyperscalers now have a cost and a security reason to treat their own hardware churn as a mine.
