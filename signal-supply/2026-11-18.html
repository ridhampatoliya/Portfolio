November 18, 2026 LIDAR SENSORS

Luminar's Stock Topped a $7 Billion Market Cap on Its First Trading Day. It Just Sold Its Lidar Business for $33 Million.

Luminar was the most famous name in American lidar — the laser sensors that give self-driving cars and robotaxis their 3D vision. It went public via a SPAC merger in December 2020 at a reported $3.4 billion valuation with Volvo as its flagship partner, and its stock market cap topped $7 billion on day one of trading. On December 15, 2025, after Volvo walked away as a customer and Luminar missed debt payments, it filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy. Its lidar business sold off in bankruptcy court for $33 million. The buyers weren't picking over a failed technology — they were picking over the one lidar architecture that couldn't be mass-manufactured as cheaply as China's.

Key takeaway Lidar comes in two architectures that measure distance the identical way — timing how long a laser pulse takes to bounce back — but build the beam-steering very differently. Mechanical lidar spins a laser/detector assembly on a motor; solid-state lidar steers the beam electronically on a flat chip with no moving parts, so it can be mass-produced on the same high-volume lines China already runs for smartphone cameras and MEMS microphones. Depending on the year and the measure, Chinese suppliers — led by Hesai and RoboSense — have supplied somewhere between roughly two-thirds and more than four-fifths of the world's lidar units. Luminar bet on the harder, costlier mechanical path. Now Uber is paying Rivian up to $1.25 billion to field robotaxis, and Rivian is reportedly weighing building its "own" lidar domestically — possibly with a Chinese partner.

It's accurate and rugged, but every unit needs precision motors, bearings, and hand calibration. That's the opposite of a process you can run at chip-fab volumes, and it was Luminar's approach.

The plain version

Think of lidar as a bat's echolocation, but with laser light instead of sound. A sensor fires rapid pulses of invisible laser light; some of that light bounces off a car or a curb and comes back. Because light travels at a fixed, known speed, the sensor can time that round trip down to billionths of a second and work out exactly how far away everything around it is, thousands of times a second, building a constantly updating 3D map of the road.

Early self-driving pioneers like Luminar built that sensor the hard way: a spinning mechanical turret, with a laser and detector mounted on a rotating head that has to stay precisely balanced while whirling around dozens of times per second. It's accurate, but every unit needs careful hand assembly and calibration — like a wind-up watch, not a printed circuit board.

Chinese manufacturers Hesai and RoboSense made a different bet: solid-state lidar, where the beam gets steered electronically on a flat chip instead of a spinning motor. No motor, no bearings, nothing to balance — just etch it like any other chip and stamp out millions. That manufacturing edge is a big reason Chinese suppliers have ended up making somewhere between two-thirds and more than four-fifths of the world's lidar units, depending on who's counting and which year.

Luminar bet on the pricier mechanical approach and partnered with Volvo. When Volvo walked away in late 2025, Luminar collapsed into bankruptcy within weeks — a company whose stock once valued it above $7 billion sold its entire lidar business for $33 million. Now the stakes are rising: Uber is paying Rivian up to $1.25 billion to build tens of thousands of robotaxis, and robotaxis need a lot of cheap, reliable lidar. US lawmakers don't want Chinese-made sensors feeding what an American self-driving car can "see" — so Rivian is reportedly weighing building its own lidar domestically, possibly in partnership with a Chinese firm. Sensors turned out to be the new chips: whoever can mass-manufacture them like a chip wins.

The expert version

Lidar ranges by time-of-flight (ToF): a pulsed laser, typically at 905nm or 1550nm, emits a pulse; a photodetector captures the return; distance is computed as d = c·t/2, where c is the speed of light and t is round-trip time. A target 60 meters out returns its pulse in roughly 400 nanoseconds — the timing precision the whole system has to hit, repeatedly, across a wide field of view, to assemble a usable point cloud.

The architectural fork is in how the beam covers that field of view. Mechanical spinning lidar — Velodyne's original 64-beam design, and Luminar's own architecture — mounts multiple laser/detector pairs on a rotating assembly, transferring power and data across the rotating interface via slip rings or optical couplers. That yields a dense, reliable 360° field of view, but it requires precision motors, bearings, and tight mechanical tolerances assembled largely by hand — a process that doesn't scale the way chip fabrication does.

Solid-state alternatives eliminate the rotating assembly entirely. MEMS lidar, RoboSense's core approach, uses microscopic mirrors etched via standard semiconductor lithography that oscillate electrically to steer the beam; flash lidar illuminates the whole scene at once with no steering at all. Both can run on largely the same high-yield, high-volume fab lines China already operates for smartphone camera modules and MEMS microphones — which is why Hesai and RoboSense could ramp into the hundreds of thousands to low millions of units a year while mechanical-architecture builders stayed in the tens of thousands. S&P Global Mobility put Chinese suppliers above 80% of global lidar sales in 2023; other breakdowns by year and segment put Hesai, RoboSense, and Huawei combined closer to two-thirds to three-quarters — the exact figure moves, the direction doesn't.

Luminar, which went public via a roughly $3.4 billion SPAC merger in December 2020 with Volvo as its design-win partner, filed Chapter 11 in the US Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of Texas (Case No. 25-90807) on December 15, 2025, after Volvo terminated its supply agreement and Luminar missed debt payments. Its lidar assets sold to MicroVision for $33 million; a separate semiconductor unit sold to Quantum Computing Inc. for $110 million. Meanwhile, Uber has committed up to $1.25 billion through 2031 to help Rivian field autonomous vehicles, starting with roughly 10,000 robotaxis in San Francisco and Miami in 2028. Reuters reported in May 2026 that Rivian is in active talks to build lidar domestically, "possibly in partnership with" a Chinese firm, after US lawmakers raised national-security concerns about Chinese-made sensors feeding perception data into American autonomous vehicles. Reshoring final assembly doesn't necessarily reshore the solid-state manufacturing process advantage that made the Chinese approach cheap in the first place.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: Robotaxi sensors are running the same cheap-mass-manufacturing playbook that already won memory, solar, and batteries for China — and "reshoring" may end up meaning relocated assembly, not a relocated advantage.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: A manufacturing-architecture choice, not a sensing breakthrough, decided who supplies the "eyes" for the next decade of autonomous vehicles — and that choice is now colliding with US national-security review of robotaxi supply chains.
