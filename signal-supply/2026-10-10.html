October 10, 2026 EMERGENCY POWER

The US Government Has Let PJM Switch On AI Data Centers' Diesel Backup Generators as Grid Power Four Times in 2026. Two Days Ago, PJM Told Its Members to Expect a Fifth, This Winter.

Section 202(c) of the Federal Power Act lets the Department of Energy override normal grid rules during a reliability emergency. In 2026, Washington has used that power four separate times to let PJM — the grid operator for 13 states and D.C. — direct AI data centers to switch to their own diesel backup generators, not to keep their own servers running, but to take load off the shared grid for everyone else. The latest briefing, filed two days before this edition, is about getting ready to do it again this winter.

Key takeaway DOE issued at least four Section 202(c) emergency orders in 2026 authorizing PJM to dispatch data centers' backup diesel generators as last-resort grid power: Order 202-26-06 (Jan. 26), 202-26-23 (May 18), 202-26-32 (June 30), and 202-26-41 (Sept. 1). On October 8, PJM briefed its Operating Committee on all four as it prepares for winter — a season NERC's own risk assessment already flags as elevated, largely because of data-center demand growth.

The plain version

Every big data center keeps a fleet of diesel generators parked out back — insurance against a blackout, meant to run a few hours a year at most, mostly just for testing. In 2026, the US government started treating those generators as something else: a backup power plant for the whole neighborhood.

Here's the mechanism. Section 202(c) of a 90-year-old law lets the Department of Energy declare a grid emergency and temporarily override the normal rules. Four separate times this year — during a January deep freeze, a May heat emergency, the record-breaking Fourth of July weekend heat wave, and again in September — DOE used that power to let PJM, which runs the grid across 13 states and Washington, D.C., tell data centers and other huge electricity users to flip on their own diesel generators. Not to keep their own servers running through an outage, which is what the generators are normally for, but to take load off the shared grid so everyone else's lights stay on right as demand peaks.

It worked, visibly. During the July episode, PJM's demand hit a record 166,304 megawatts, breaking a mark that had stood since 2006, and residents near Ashburn, Virginia — home to the world's densest cluster of data centers — posted photos of black diesel smoke drifting over their neighborhoods on a 102°F day.

Two days before this edition, PJM briefed its members on all four 2026 orders while prepping for winter — a season grid watchdogs already call higher-risk than usual, largely because of data centers. The generators bought as insurance have quietly become a load-bearing part of the grid's actual plan.

The expert version

The legal lever is Federal Power Act Section 202(c), which lets DOE issue short-term emergency orders directing generation and transmission assets to operate outside their normal permitted parameters — overriding environmental limits, interconnection rules, or standard economic dispatch — when grid reliability is at risk. DOE issued at least four 202(c) orders in 2026 that specifically authorize PJM to direct "backup generation resources" at data centers and other large loads (generally facilities of 50 MW or more) to self-generate as a last resort before, or during, a declared Energy Emergency Alert Level 3 — PJM's highest alert tier, one step short of controlled load shedding: Order 202-26-06 (Jan. 26, Winter Storm Fern), 202-26-23 (May 18, heat emergency), 202-26-32 (June 30, the heat wave that drove demand to a record 166,304 MW, past the 165,563 MW set in 2006), and 202-26-41 (Sept. 1). A separate set of 2026 orders (202-26-17, -24, -40) uses the same authority to keep specific utility-owned plants, like Constellation's Eddystone station, running past retirement — same statute, different asset class.

The emissions side is genuinely disputed, not just politically contested. Virginia's Department of Environmental Quality has begun treating these DOE-triggered dispatches as "emergency" operation, exempting them from the stricter annual run-hour and permit limits that apply to routine testing — a reclassification critics call a loophole. A Virginia legislative audit found data-center diesel generators account for under 4% of the region's NOx emissions. A Washington Post analysis built on Virginia Commonwealth University and University of Washington modeling of 132 Northern Virginia facilities estimated that, even run under an hour a week on average, their combined public-health burden is comparable to roughly five gas plants the size of the regional Possum Point station, with on the order of three or more associated premature deaths a year. Those two figures aren't measuring the same thing — emissions share versus modeled health burden — and neither has displaced the other; treat the gap as unresolved, not as one side being simply wrong.

The structural driver isn't going away. NERC's most recent winter reliability assessment already rated seven North American regions at elevated blackout risk, citing roughly 20 GW of new winter demand against under 10 GW of new capacity, with data centers named as the primary driver — which is why PJM's Oct. 8 "preparation for winter" briefing to its Operating Committee reads less like a retrospective and more like a forecast. Separately, the backup-generator supply chain is now a bottleneck of its own: Generac's data-center order backlog reached $1.6 billion in Q2 2026 (including $1 billion in new orders inside 90 days), and Caterpillar's power-generation segment revenue rose 41% year over year in Q1 2026 to $2.8 billion, both driven overwhelmingly by data-center demand.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: backup diesel generators bought for occasional blackouts have quietly become a real-time grid resource that regulators now expect to call on repeatedly — which turns AI's buildout pace into everyone else's electricity-reliability problem.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: Section 202(c) has become a recurring, low-friction policy lever for converting idle behind-the-meter diesel capacity into grid-dispatchable reserve, which both masks and defers the underlying generation-vs-load-growth gap that NERC says data centers are driving.
