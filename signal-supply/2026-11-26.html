AI Hardware Thefts Have Topped $150 Million This Year. 92% of the Heists Never Broke a Single Lock.

Cargo-security firm Overhaul has tracked more than $150 million in stolen AI servers, GPUs, and data-center gear in 2026 — and in 92% of the high-value cases it analyzed, nobody cut a fence or picked a lock. They forged a name on a piece of paper.

Key takeaway The AI buildout's most exposed link isn't the fab, the export-control rule, or the GPU die itself — it's the thirty seconds a freight broker spends checking a registration number in a database before handing a $2 million trailer to whoever shows up holding the paperwork.

The other four are a paperwork and verification problem, which is exactly why Overhaul found deception, not force, in 92% of the high-value tech-shipment thefts it analyzed this year.

The plain version

Picture a moving scam, not a break-in. A company needs to ship a trailer full of AI servers from Dallas to Reno, worth a couple of million dollars, so it posts the job on a digital freight marketplace — basically a job board for trucking. A legitimate hauler bids the market rate and picks it up. But so can someone who isn't a hauler at all: a fraud ring that has "borrowed" a real, dormant trucking company's government registration number — the trucking world's version of a stolen ID — and uses it to underbid everyone else and grab the job fast.

The freight broker handing out the load checks that registration number against an online government database, confirms it's active and insured, and awards the job. What that quick check can't confirm is who actually shows up to drive. A driver working for the fraud ring arrives, loads the cargo, signs the paperwork, and then simply disappears: tracker switched off, truck never arrives where it's supposed to. The servers resurface later on resale markets, often overseas, selling for roughly double what they cost new.

It's called double-brokering, and in 2026 it's the single biggest reason AI hardware vanishes before it reaches a data center: more than $150 million worth so far, according to the freight-security firm Overhaul, with 92% of cases involving a forged identity rather than a broken lock. One group even stole two Nvidia-branded trailers in October, purely because of the logo on the side, and got away with 20,000 pounds of sand.

The expert version

The freight-brokerage system runs on a verification shortcut that scales well for honest shippers and terribly against fraud. A legitimate carrier registers for FMCSA operating authority — a Motor Carrier (MC) number — checkable for free in the public SAFER database for active status, insurance filings, and safety rating. Brokers treat a clean SAFER record as sufficient proof of identity. "Double brokering" exploits the gap between that record and physical reality: a fraud ring obtains or clones a real, often-dormant carrier's MC number and insurance paperwork, bids below market on a load posted to a digital load board, and gets it assigned by a broker who has verified the number but never the person standing in the lot. The Transportation Intermediaries Association has documented loads re-brokered through three or four intermediaries before the cargo reaches whoever actually moves it, and FMCSA has told Congress it still lacks the data and civil-penalty authority to police the practice systematically.

Overhaul's AI Infrastructure Intel Report, released October 21, 2026, quantifies how disproportionately AI hardware draws this treatment: over $150 million in data-center equipment, compute components, and chips stolen in cargo transit so far this year, up 34% since 2024, with deception — fraudulent carrier identities, double-brokering, spoofed dispatch communications, falsified bills of lading — present in 92% of the high-value technology-shipment thefts it analyzed. The company reports the largest single case topped $100 million, and that for every dollar of stolen hardware that lands on an insurance claim, roughly three more dollars are lost to costs that never appear on one: production schedule slips, compliance exposure, rush-ordered replacement freight. The incentive math is blunt — a single populated AI server rack can run close to $3 million, against the same unverified handoff and the same fraud effort a trailer of ordinary consumer electronics would take.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: The AI buildout's most exposed link isn't the fab, the export rule, or the GPU itself — it's the thirty seconds a broker spends checking a number in a database before handing cargo to whoever's holding it.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: Identity-verification gaps in domestic freight brokerage, not export-control evasion or physical hijacking, are now the dominant loss vector for AI hardware in transit — a risk category most chip and server buyers aren't underwriting for.
