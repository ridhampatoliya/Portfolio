November 19, 2026 PHOTORESIST

Three Japanese Companies Supply Up to 85% of the World's EUV Photoresist. An Oil Blockade 2,000 Miles Away Nearly Choked Off What It's Made From.

ASML's lithography machines get all the attention as chipmaking's single point of failure. But earlier this year, the thing that actually rattled Samsung's and SK Hynix's most advanced chip lines wasn't a $400 million lithography tool — it was a light-sensitive liquid painted onto the wafer before that machine even switches on, and the oil tankers that make its key ingredient.

Key takeaway Photoresist isn't scarce because the chemistry is exotic — it's scarce because almost all of the EUV-grade formula is made by three Japanese companies, using an ultra-pure solvent cracked from naphtha, a byproduct of the same Middle Eastern crude oil that stopped flowing smoothly once the Israel-Iran war spilled into the Strait of Hormuz in early March 2026. Switching chemical suppliers mid-crisis doesn't help fast: requalifying a new photoresist source takes on the order of a year, longer at leading-edge nodes — so even a short disruption upstream can take a year to fully absorb downstream.

Since the Israel-Iran war spilled into the Strait of Hormuz in early March 2026, the waterway that normally carries roughly a quarter of the world's seaborne oil (and about a fifth of its LNG) has been effectively closed to ordinary shipping. That's where this chain starts — a chip chemical's supply problem that began as an oil-tanker problem.

The plain version

Before a chipmaking laser ever touches a silicon wafer, the wafer gets coated with a thin layer of liquid called photoresist — think of it like a light-sensitive paint. Wherever light hits it, through a stencil-like mask, the chemistry changes, so a later step can wash away exactly the right spots and carve out the pattern of a chip's billions of transistors. The $400 million EUV machines that fire that light get all the headlines. The paint rarely does.

It should. Three Japanese companies — JSR, Tokyo Ohka Kogyo, and Shin-Etsu Chemical — are estimated to supply as much as 85% of the world's EUV-grade photoresist. You can't just swap to a different brand mid-crisis, either: a new resist formula needs roughly a year of testing before a fab trusts it on wafers that can cost tens of thousands of dollars each.

Here's the twist that connects a chip chemical to an oil war: photoresist (along with related coatings and the glue used to stack memory chips) is made using an ultra-pure solvent that's cracked from naphtha — a byproduct of crude oil refining. Japan's photoresist industry gets more than 40% of that naphtha from the Middle East. When the Israel-Iran war spilled into the Strait of Hormuz in early March 2026 and the strait effectively closed to normal shipping, six of Japan's twelve naphtha-cracking plants reportedly cut output. By late April, Shin-Etsu, TOK, and JSR had reportedly warned Samsung and SK Hynix that their raw-material supply could be disrupted, and said they were weighing alternate solvent sources in South Korea or China — which, again, restarts that year-long qualification clock.

Chipmakers reportedly held about six months of buffer stock when that warning went out. Whether that buffer has since run dry, nobody's independently confirmed as of this writing. Either way, the lesson holds: the fragile link in advanced chipmaking wasn't the lithography machine. It was a jar of liquid chemistry, three steps removed from an oil tanker stuck in a queue.

The expert version

Photoresist is a photosensitive polymer formulation spin-coated onto a wafer prior to exposure. The dominant chemistry for both EUV (13.5nm) and ArF-immersion deep-UV (193nm) lithography is the chemically amplified resist (CAR): exposure generates acid catalytically, which then drives polymer cross-linking or chain scission during a post-exposure bake, amplifying a small absorbed-photon signal into a large solubility contrast that development (typically a TMAH solution) can resolve. EUV resist chemistry faces an added constraint — the resolution/line-width-roughness/sensitivity (RLS) trilemma — because comparatively few EUV photons are absorbed per exposed feature despite their higher individual energy (~92 eV), pushing stochastic defectivity higher. That's part of why alternative chemistries, like the tin-oxide metal-oxide resists (MOR) JSR acquired via Inpria, are being pursued alongside conventional CAR for EUV.

Formulating CAR or MOR photoresist — plus the bottom anti-reflective coatings (BARC), spin-on hardmasks (SOH), and temporary bonding adhesives used to stack HBM dies — depends on ultra-high-purity propylene glycol monomethyl ether and its acetate (PGME/PGMEA) as carrier solvent. PGME/PGMEA is produced from propylene oxide, itself derived from naphtha cracking. One industry estimate puts JSR, Tokyo Ohka Kogyo (TOK), and Shin-Etsu Chemical at close to 85% of global EUV photoresist production volume; a separate, older estimate puts the top four Japanese suppliers (adding Fujifilm) at roughly 76% of the broader photoresist market in 2023. The figures aren't directly comparable, but both describe extreme concentration in a small number of Japanese formulators.

Since early March 2026, the Strait of Hormuz has been effectively closed to normal shipping after the Israel-Iran war spilled into the strait, curtailing Middle Eastern crude and naphtha feedstock. By late April, TrendForce reported Japan's photoresist supply chain's reliance on Middle East naphtha at over 40%, and multiple Asian outlets (SCMP, Seoul Economic Daily, BigGo Finance via The Elec) reported six of Japan's twelve naphtha-cracking centers had cut output, with Shin-Etsu, TOK, and JSR warning Samsung and SK Hynix of potential PGME/PGMEA disruption — EUV-dependent leading-edge nodes flagged as most exposed. Analysts cited typical chipmaker safety-stock buffers of roughly six months as of that reporting. I found no independently confirmed reporting past April 2026 on whether that buffer has since been exhausted or the naphtha flow has normalized, so the current severity should be treated as unresolved rather than settled. Requalifying an alternate PGME/PGMEA source, per multiple reports, takes on the order of a year, longer at leading-edge nodes — meaning even a brief feedstock disruption can propagate into a year-long supply-chain adjustment.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: The thing standing between a $400 million EUV machine and a working chip can be a chemical formula three companies control — and that formula runs on oil that has to cross a strait a war can close.
