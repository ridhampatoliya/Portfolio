October 29, 2026 AEROSPACE CASTINGS

A Bad Batch of Metal Powder Has Grounded as Many as 835 Jets. GE Just Paid $11.75 Billion to Buy the Bottleneck Behind It.

Turning molten nickel into a jet engine's hottest, hardest-working part takes a handful of furnaces on Earth that can do it to aerospace spec. One contaminated batch of metal powder showed exactly how few that handful is — and now the industry's biggest engine maker just bought one of them outright rather than keep waiting in line.

Key takeaway Pratt & Whitney's geared turbofan engines have been dogged since 2023 by a contaminated lot of metal powder used to make turbine disks and blades. At the worst point tracked, Cirium data put 835 Pratt & Whitney-powered jets grounded worldwide awaiting inspection or repair. The underlying fix — more casting and forging capacity — can't be rushed: the dominant independent supplier says meaningful new capacity won't exist before 2028. So on September 8, 2026, GE Aerospace agreed to pay $11.75 billion, its largest deal since splitting off from General Electric, to buy Consolidated Precision Products, a maker of exactly these parts. When you can't buy more of a bottleneck, you buy the bottleneck.

The plain version

Every jet engine has a spinning disk and a ring of blades that live inside roughly 2,000°F combustion gas while spinning tens of thousands of times a minute. To survive that, those parts aren't melted and poured like a candlestick. The metal — a nickel "superalloy" — is first turned into a fine powder, then squeezed and heated under enormous pressure in a process called hot isostatic pressing, almost like baking a part out of metal dust instead of casting it from liquid. Only a small number of factories on the planet can do this reliably to aerospace quality.

That narrowness became a real problem. Starting in 2023, inspectors traced tiny, invisible contamination in some of that metal powder — microscopic voids that could eventually crack under stress. Airlines had no choice but to pull engines, and sometimes whole planes, out of service for inspection and rework. At the worst point tracked, the data firm Cirium counted as many as 835 Pratt & Whitney-powered jets sitting grounded worldwide, waiting on parts or repairs — while Boeing and Airbus together are still sitting on more than 16,000 unbuilt orders.

The fix nobody can rush is casting and forging capacity. Building a new furnace or press that can handle this work takes years, and the dominant independent supplier, Howmet Aerospace, has told investors that meaningful new capacity won't exist before 2028. So instead of waiting in line, GE Aerospace just spent $11.75 billion — its biggest deal since splitting off from General Electric in 2024 — to buy Consolidated Precision Products outright, a company that makes exactly these parts. When you can't buy more of a bottleneck, you buy the bottleneck.

The expert version

Turbine disks and blades operate in a jet engine's hottest, most mechanically loaded zone, so they're made from nickel-based superalloys processed via powder metallurgy rather than conventional casting: superalloy is gas-atomized into fine powder, sealed in a container, then consolidated under hot isostatic pressing (HIP) — typically well above 100 MPa and roughly 1,100–1,200°C simultaneously — to produce a near-net-shape billet with minimal porosity, which is then forged and precision-machined to final tolerance. The process is chosen because it limits the segregation and grain-boundary defects a cast part would carry, but it depends on powder free of inclusions, since even a sub-millimeter contaminant can nucleate a fatigue crack over thousands of flight cycles.

That's effectively what hit Pratt & Whitney's Geared Turbofan (GTF) family starting in 2023: a contaminated lot of powder metal was traced through the supply chain, triggering a mandatory, rolling inspection and overhaul program across the in-service fleet. Cirium data cited by industry trackers put the global count of stored GTF-powered aircraft at 835 at the end of October 2025, up from 748 at mid-year; RTX (Pratt & Whitney's parent) said in April 2026 the figure was trending down as shop throughput improved, though a precise, more current count wasn't available in the sources reviewed for this piece — treat the downward trend as directionally reported, not confirmed.

The structural issue outlasts any single defect lot. Casting and forging capacity for aerospace-grade superalloy components is concentrated in a handful of independent foundries, chiefly Howmet Aerospace and Consolidated Precision Products ("CPP"), and Howmet has told investors that meaningful new press and furnace capacity won't come online before 2028–2030, since the equipment itself takes two to three-plus years to build. Against a combined Boeing-Airbus order backlog north of 16,000 aircraft as of July 2026 (Forecast International) and GE Aerospace's own projection of roughly 30% growth in airfoil demand by 2030, GE chose to internalize the chokepoint rather than compete for it: its September 8, 2026 agreement to acquire CPP for $11.75 billion — financed with $7 billion cash plus new debt, expected to close in the second half of 2027 pending antitrust review — is, per CEO H. Lawrence Culp Jr., explicitly about securing "mission-critical" casting capacity.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: The planes airlines can't get aren't stuck on an assembly line — they're stuck waiting on a handful of furnaces, and GE just bought one instead of waiting in line like everyone else.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: Aerospace-grade casting and forging capacity, not final assembly, is the structural chokepoint behind both the GTF grounding crisis and the industry's order backlog — forcing OEMs to vertically integrate suppliers rather than compete for capacity on the open market.
