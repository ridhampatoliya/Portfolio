October 12, 2026 DRONE TARIFFS

Washington Just Taxed Chinese Drones at 100%. It Still Can't Define What Counts as Not Chinese.

On August 13, 2026, the U.S. did two things that look like they contradict each other, on the same day. It loosened export rules so American drone makers could sell more easily abroad, and it taxed drones and parts coming in at up to 100%, aimed squarely at an industry one Chinese company, DJI, is estimated to dominate with roughly 70% of the global market. Weeks later, the rule meant to spare allied manufacturers from that full rate still has no definition of what actually qualifies as spared.

The plain version

On one day this past August, Washington did two things that look like they contradict each other. It made it easier for American drone companies to sell their drones overseas. And on that same day, it slapped a tax of up to 100% on drones and drone parts coming into the U.S., aimed squarely at Chinese-made hardware, since one company, DJI, is estimated to build something like 7 out of every 10 drones sold worldwide.

The logic: make foreign, especially Chinese, drones expensive enough and American buyers switch to U.S.-made ones, pulling factories here instead of Shenzhen. Friendly countries, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, the EU, the UK, get a much lower rate, as low as 10%, but only if their drones are built from what the rule calls "substantially all" non-Chinese parts.

Here's the catch nobody can answer yet: the government never defined what "substantially all" means. Fifty-one percent of the cost? Ninety? Does one Chinese-made camera sensor disqualify an otherwise-American drone? Weeks after the rule took effect, allied drone makers still don't know which tariff bracket they'll land in, because almost every drone, wherever it's assembled, still depends on a global parts bin China dominates: batteries, motors, flight-control chips, camera sensors.

And eight days before any of this, China moved first, tightening its own export licensing on drone components headed to the U.S. That squeezes the very supply of parts that would let anyone, American or allied, build a genuinely "Chinese-free" drone. Three trade levers, pulling on one small, overlooked industry, all at once.

The expert version

Two federal actions landed on the same day, August 13, 2026. First, the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) finalized a rule narrowing ECCN 9A012, the export-control classification covering most unmanned aircraft, so only drones with 3+ hours of flight endurance stay subject to the strictest national-security licensing, and dropped wind-gust tolerance as a control parameter. That eases export friction for the bulk of commercial and prosumer drones U.S. manufacturers want to sell to allies.

Second, under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act, Proclamation 11055 imposed a tiered tariff on imported drones and critical components: 100% on drones over 25kg takeoff weight, on thermal-imaging-equipped drones, and on docking stations, effective September 3, 2026; a second 25% tier plus component-level tariffs phase in February 9, 2027. Treaty partners, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, Switzerland, Liechtenstein, the EU, are capped at 15%, the UK at 10%, conditioned on a rule-of-origin test requiring "substantially all" critical components (flight controller, propulsion, batteries, optical payload, RF/comms) to originate in a qualifying country. As of the latest published guidance, Commerce has not issued the implementing regulation defining that threshold, leaving allied manufacturers unable to calculate, ahead of shipment, which bracket applies to a given SKU.

Eight days earlier, China's Ministry of Commerce issued Announcement No. 34, effective August 5, moving drone-related dual-use exports to the U.S. from general license to mandatory case-by-case review, tightening the component spigot (motors, cameras, lithium cells, flight-control ICs) both Chinese assemblers and many "allied" drone builders still draw from, since DJI alone holds an estimated 70%+ share of the global consumer and prosumer market, with much of its upstream parts base concentrated in the same Pearl River Delta cluster regardless of where final assembly happens.

The result mirrors the semiconductor playbook, export licensing, tariff tiers, rules of origin, applied to a far less vertically integrated industry, testing whether "substantially all" can even be verified at the component level before the next tariff tier phases in.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: the newest front in the chip-style trade war isn't chips, it's the camera and the flight-control board inside a drone, and the rulebook for which ones count as safe to buy didn't exist before the tariffs did.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: Washington is running export-license-plus-tariff-tier-plus-rule-of-origin policy, the same three-lever structure used against Chinese chips, against a far more fragmented industry, without having published the certification standard the system depends on.
