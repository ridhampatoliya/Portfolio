November 10, 2026 AEROSPACE METALS

A Handful of Companies Are Qualified to Supply Aerospace-Grade Titanium. Boeing Struck the Biggest One From Its List in 2022 — and Forged Paperwork From Another Supplier Still Got Fake Titanium Onto Real Jets.

A titanium shortage doesn't look like an empty shelf. It looks like forged paperwork making it all the way onto a real airplane, because the list of suppliers trusted enough to make the real thing got too short to meet demand.

Key takeaway Russia's VSMPO-AVISMA — partly owned by state defense conglomerate Rostec — was reportedly the source of roughly a third of Boeing's titanium and about half of Airbus's before 2022. Days after the invasion of Ukraine, Boeing struck it from its approved-supplier list; Airbus kept buying for a time while diversifying. Qualifying a brand-new source for aerospace use can take 3–5+ years, and the short list of alternatives (mainly in Japan, Kazakhstan, and Saudi Arabia) hasn't grown fast enough to fill the gap. In 2024, the FAA opened an investigation after forged certificates let titanium from an unverified Chinese source — funneled through intermediaries — onto real Boeing and Airbus jets.

The plain version

Titanium is everywhere in aerospace because it's strong, light, and doesn't corrode — jet engines, landing gear, airframes. But not just any titanium will do. Parts that can't fail need "aerospace-grade" titanium sponge, made at a small number of tightly controlled plants, each one taking years to prove trustworthy enough that a plane maker will stake lives on its metal.

Before 2022, the single biggest aerospace-grade titanium supplier on Earth was a Russian company, VSMPO-AVISMA, partly owned by Russia's state defense conglomerate. It reportedly supplied roughly a third of Boeing's titanium and about half of Airbus's. When Russia invaded Ukraine, Boeing cut the company from its approved-supplier list within days. Airbus kept buying for a while, insisting it was staying within sanctions rules, while working to diversify its own supply.

Here's the problem: getting a brand-new supplier "aerospace-qualified" — proven reliable enough to trust with parts that can't fail — can take three to five years or more. So the short list of alternatives, mostly in Japan, Kazakhstan, and Saudi Arabia, hasn't grown fast enough to fully replace what was lost. That thin, stretched supply chain is exactly the kind of gap counterfeiters exploit: in 2024, the FAA opened an investigation after forged paperwork let titanium that didn't really trace back to the company it claimed, move through intermediaries and onto real Boeing and Airbus jets.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: The same grade of titanium is in your pocket too — Apple used it for the iPhone 15 Pro's and iPhone Air's frame. Jet engines and smartphones are quietly drawing on the same short list of trusted suppliers.

The expert version

Titanium sponge is produced via the Kroll process — titanium tetrachloride reduced with magnesium under inert atmosphere into porous metallic "sponge," later melted into ingots. Aerospace qualification is distinct from commercial-grade certification: it requires certified chemistry and microstructure control (titanium's fatigue properties are highly sensitive to trace oxygen, nitrogen, and iron content), plus traceable chain-of-custody documentation under aerospace quality systems, because airframe and engine parts are certified against a specific melt source, not just a material specification.

VSMPO-AVISMA, roughly 25%-owned by Russia's state defense conglomerate Rostec, became the dominant global supplier of aerospace-grade titanium sponge after diversifying into Western commercial aviation in the 1990s. Pre-2022 reporting put its share at roughly a third of Boeing's titanium and about half of Airbus's overall titanium needs. On March 7, 2022, Boeing struck VSMPO from its approved-supplier list, and the companies' Ural Boeing Manufacturing joint venture subsequently suspended operations; Airbus continued sourcing Russian titanium both directly and via first-tier suppliers for a period, since Western sanctions never directly targeted VSMPO-AVISMA itself.

The qualified alternative pool remains small: Toho Titanium and Osaka Titanium in Japan, UKTMP in Kazakhstan, and ATTM in Saudi Arabia are the main non-Russian aerospace-qualified sponge producers. Industry estimates put new-supplier qualification timelines at roughly three years for standard-grade sponge and five-plus years for premium "Rhodes"-grade sponge used in the most demanding rotating engine parts. UKTMP's own sponge output fell from an estimated 19,000 tonnes in 2024 toward roughly 13,000 tonnes in 2026, attributed to broader weakness across the aircraft supply chain rather than falling titanium demand.

That thin margin is where fraud surfaced: in 2024 the FAA opened an investigation after Italy-based Titanium International Group flagged irregular certificates on titanium that had moved from an unnamed Chinese supplier, through Turkish Aerospace Industries and Spirit AeroSystems, into Boeing and Airbus airframes — with falsified paperwork claiming the metal came from Baoji Titanium Industry, a legitimate producer. Subsequent testing reportedly found the metal met the correct specification despite the forged chain of custody, but the episode showed how little slack a qualification-constrained supply chain has for verifying where aerospace metal actually originated.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: Apple's iPhone 15 Pro, Pro Max, and iPhone Air frames use Grade 5 titanium (Ti-6Al-4V) — the same alloy grade as airframe and engine components — and one analysis estimated the design could add up to 10,000 tonnes of titanium demand; consumer electronics and aerospace are now drawing on overlapping, qualification-constrained upstream capacity.
