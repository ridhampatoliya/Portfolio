November 12, 2026 TRADE

China Extended Its Rare-Earth Truce by 61 Days. The Rule That Actually Controls Supply Was Never Paused.

On November 9, Beijing pushed the deadline on its 2025 rare-earth export-control truce from November 10 to January 10, 2027 — a rollover, not a resolution. The licensing regime doing the daily work of restricting dysprosium, terbium, and five other elements was never suspended at all, and a second, unrelated pause lapses two weeks before the new deadline even arrives.

Key takeaway China's Ministry of Commerce (MOFCOM) issued Announcement No. 58 of 2026 on November 9, extending the one-year suspension of six October 2025 export-control measures (Announcements 55–58, 61, and 62 of 2025) for 61 more days, through January 10, 2027, instead of letting it expire on November 10 as originally scheduled. That suspension traces back to the US-China trade framework reached in Kuala Lumpur in October 2025. A separate suspension — of Article 2 of Announcement No. 46 of 2024, paused under Announcement No. 72 of 2025 — runs on its own clock and lapses November 27, 2026, with no confirmed extension yet. Meanwhile, the underlying licensing requirement for seven elements, in force since April 2025, was never part of either truce and has not moved.

The plain version

Think of China's rare-earth export rules as a breaker panel, not one single switch. Seven specific elements — samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium, and yttrium — have needed an individual export license since April 2025. That breaker has stayed flipped on every single day since. It was never part of any truce.

Then last October, Beijing added a second, much bigger set of breakers: six new rules, including one aimed outward at the rest of the world — any foreign-made product, anywhere, containing even trace amounts of Chinese-origin rare earths could need a Chinese export license just to leave its own country. That rule never actually switched on. Within days, as part of the broader US-China trade truce struck in Kuala Lumpur, China froze the whole package for a year, through November 10, 2026.

On November 9 — three days before this newsletter — that one-year freeze was about to run out. Instead of letting the rules snap back on, or scrapping them for good, China extended the freeze by exactly 61 days, to January 10, 2027. Not resolved. Rescheduled.

And there's a third breaker, on a completely different rule, frozen on its own separate clock — it lapses November 27, with no word yet on whether it gets the same treatment.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: carmakers, chipmakers, and defense contractors who need dysprosium magnets and terbium alloys now have two more deadlines to track instead of one — and the breaker doing the actual daily restricting was never touched at all.

The expert version

China's rare-earth export-control regime runs on two legally distinct tracks, and only one of them moved this week. Track one is the permanent baseline: since April 2025, MOFCOM and the General Administration of Customs (GACC) have required individual export licenses for seven elements — samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium, and yttrium — plus related separation and magnet-fabrication technology. Dysprosium and terbium are the dopants that let neodymium-iron-boron magnets hold coercivity at high operating temperature, which is why they sit inside EV traction motors, wind-turbine generators, and precision-guided actuators. That licensing track has run continuously since April 2025 and was not touched by this week's news.

Track two is the escalation package: Announcements No. 55, 56, 57, 58, 61, and 62 of 2025, issued in October 2025. Announcement 61 is the one trade lawyers flagged hardest — an extraterritorial "50% rule" that would require a Chinese export license for any foreign-made good, manufactured anywhere, containing Chinese-origin rare-earth value content above a threshold, structurally mirroring (if pointed outward) the logic of the US Foreign Direct Product Rule. Within days of announcing it, China suspended the entire package via Announcement No. 70 of 2025, effective November 10, 2025 through November 10, 2026, as part of the Kuala Lumpur trade-truce framework that also paused an additional 24% US tariff for one year.

On November 9, 2026, MOFCOM issued Announcement No. 58 of 2026, extending that suspension 61 days further, to January 10, 2027 — a rider on the existing freeze, not a repeal of the underlying rules. A third, separate instrument, Announcement No. 72 of 2025, paused Article 2 of Announcement No. 46 of 2024 on its own independent clock; that pause lapses November 27, 2026, and as of publication no extension has been confirmed for it — making it the nearer-term variable worth watching, developing story. Net effect: the binding, day-to-day control stayed exactly as restrictive as it was the day before; what moved was the suspension date on an escalation package nobody has actually operated under yet.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: the renewal date, not the underlying rule, is now the real pressure point in US-China tech trade — and because it resets on a roughly two-month cycle instead of annually, procurement and compliance teams need to recalendar around it far more often.
