The Panama Canal Cut Daily Transits From 36 to 32. One Ship Just Paid $5.3 Million to Skip the Queue.

Drought has trimmed the number of ships the canal lets through each day by 11% since July — right as its slot auction hits record prices. About 40% of all U.S. container traffic moves through this one waterway, and the next cut lands this week.

Spirit's owner paid a record $5.3 million for one Neopanamax slot, beating the $4.6 million record set just weeks earlier by G. Arete. The plain version

The Panama Canal isn't a sea-level ditch like Suez — it's an elevator. Locks lift each ship up over the isthmus using fresh water, then lower it back down the other side, and every drop used to do that gets flushed out to the ocean, gone for good. When it rains a lot, there's always enough water. When it doesn't, the canal authority has exactly one blunt lever: let fewer ships through per day.

This year the rains didn't show up. Water flowing into the lakes that feed the locks is down roughly 44% for the year, so the Panama Canal Authority has been quietly turning the dial down: a normal day moves 36 ships through. Since early September that's been 34. As of September 15 — literally this week — it drops again, to 32.

Two fewer ships a day sounds trivial. It isn't, because a canal has no "come back tomorrow, it's less busy" — every ship that misses today's cutoff competes with tomorrow's ships too, so a small daily shortfall compounds into a real backlog within days. Owners in a hurry (a gas tanker on a charter deadline, a container ship racing to make a holiday-season sailing) can skip that regular line entirely by bidding in the canal's daily slot auction instead of waiting. On September 1st, one gas tanker's owner paid a record $5.3 million for a single slot — beating a record set just three weeks earlier.

Why should a tech reader care about a canal? About 40% of all U.S. container traffic, and nearly half of everything shipped from Northeast Asia to the U.S. East Coast, passes through this one waterway. Squeeze it during peak holiday shipping season and the cost shows up later — in freight bills, in delivery dates, in the price of whatever gadget had to find a longer way around.

The expert version

Mechanically, the Panama Canal is a freshwater lift-lock system, not a sea-level cut. Each transit through the Gatun, Pedro Miguel, Miraflores, and Neopanamax locks consumes on the order of tens of millions of gallons of fresh water per ship, drawn from Gatun and Alajuela Lakes and ultimately sourced from rainfall over the watershed. That water is released to sea level once used; the water-saving basins added in the 2016 Neopanamax expansion recover a meaningful share of it per transit, but net consumption is still large and one-directional.

The binding constraint isn't lock throughput capacity — it's reservoir recharge. This hydrological year, the ACP reports accumulated watershed rainfall down roughly 34% and inflow into the reservoir system down roughly 44%, consistent with an intensifying El Niño, which reliably suppresses isthmus rainfall by shifting the Pacific trade-wind pattern that normally drives moisture onto the watershed. With Gatun Lake tracking below the ACP's operating curve, the agency's only short-run control variable is daily transit count: cut from a baseline of 36/day to 34/day effective early September, and to 32/day effective September 15 — an 11% reduction in two months, distinct from the draft (loading depth) restrictions used in earlier drought cycles.

The market-clearing mechanism for scarce slots is the ACP's daily transit slot auction, layered on top of the standard booking and reservation system: any operator can bid to jump the regular queue, cleared highest-bid-first. On September 1, LPG carrier G. Spirit's owner paid $5.3 million for a single Neopanamax slot — a record that itself broke a $4.6 million record set August 14. Those prices are a real-time signal of water scarcity: they rise precisely when a ship's schedule value (a charter deadline, a cargo's spot-price window, a peak-season sailing) exceeds the cost of waiting in the regular queue, which is exactly the price-discovery function an auction is designed to perform.

Roughly 40% of U.S. container volume, and about 46% of container traffic moving from Northeast Asia to the U.S. East Coast, transits this single chokepoint — meaning a capacity cut here has an outsized, geographically concentrated effect on U.S. import lead times relative to its share of global trade tonnage.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: this cut lands right at the start of peak holiday shipping season, when everything from consumer electronics to warehouse robotics parts is racing to reach U.S. shelves — a slower or pricier Panama transit now shows up as a later, more expensive delivery in November and December.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: procurement and logistics teams sourcing from Northeast Asia should model Panama transit as a variable-cost, variable-lead-time chokepoint through at least this hydrological year — treating the slot-auction price as a leading indicator of freight-rate and ETA risk on East Coast-bound electronics and hardware shipments, not a one-off anomaly.
