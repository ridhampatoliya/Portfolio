November 5, 2026 SHADOW FLEET

Sanctioned Tankers Flying Russia's Own Flag Jumped From 3% to 21% in Nine Months. That's Not the Scheme Shrinking — It's Getting Harder to Board.

A modern AI accelerator isn't the only thing built from layers designed to obscure what's inside. A Western oil-sanctions regime runs on one lever — deny Western insurance to any cargo priced too high — and a growing fleet of tankers has simply stopped needing that insurance at all. Researchers who track the fleet monthly just found its ships switching from fake foreign flags to Russia's real one. That's a harder target, not a retreating one.

Key takeaway The G7's price cap on Russian oil doesn't ban the oil — it bars Western insurers, shipowners and financiers from touching any cargo sold above $60 a barrel, on the bet that sellers have no alternative to Western-dominated marine insurance. The workaround: a fleet of aging tankers, bought through opaque shell companies and flagged with little-scrutinized registries, that never needed that insurance to begin with. The Kyiv School of Economics Institute, which tracks this fleet every month, found the share of its ships flying Russia's own flag — rather than a borrowed foreign one — jumped from 3% to 21% in nine months. A separate September 2026 study by the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air (CREA) found vessels using false foreign flags falling, from a peak of 101 in October 2025 to 45 by June 2026 — not because the scheme is shrinking, but because more ships are simply registering as genuinely Russian instead, which changes the politics of ever boarding one. Maritime-intelligence firm Windward says its tracked "dark fleet" — ships that go quiet or fake their position — passed 2,180 vessels in Q2 2026, with disappearing acts surging nearly fivefold in a single quarter. Rival tracker Kpler just answered by bolting daily satellite imagery onto its own network.

The plain version

Imagine a rule that says no Western delivery company, insurer, or truck-rental firm may help move a package worth more than a set price. That's roughly what the G7 did to Russian oil in 2022: a $60-a-barrel price cap, enforced not by physically stopping tankers at sea but by cutting off Western shipping and insurance to anyone who carries oil sold for more.

Russia's answer was simple: stop needing Western shipping and insurance. Buyers linked to Moscow quietly bought up old, often near-retirement tankers — frequently through shell companies registered somewhere that doesn't ask who really owns a ship — then registered them under obscure flag registries that rarely inspect anything. A fleet assembled this way was never going to need a Western insurer's blessing, so the price cap simply doesn't touch it.

Researchers who track this fleet every month found something telling: the share of these tankers now flying Russia's actual flag, instead of a borrowed foreign one, jumped from 3% to 21% in under a year. That matters because boarding a ship that's lying about its flag is one kind of confrontation; boarding a ship that's honestly flying Russia's own flag is another, riskier one. A separate study found the number of tankers using fake foreign flags has actually fallen — not because the scheme shrank, but because more ships stopped bothering to hide and just registered as Russian.

These tankers also increasingly vanish from tracking altogether, switching off or faking the location beacon every ship is required to broadcast. One tracking firm counted its "dark fleet" pass 2,180 vessels this spring, with disappearing acts jumping nearly fivefold in a single quarter. So trackers are fighting back with more satellites. Neither side has won.

The expert version

The G7/EU/Australia price cap, effective since December 5, 2022, is a services-denial mechanism rather than a trade embargo: it bars firms domiciled in capping jurisdictions — shipowners, P&I (protection and indemnity) insurers, reinsurers, and financiers — from servicing Russian-origin seaborne crude sold above $60/barrel. Because the bulk of global marine liability cover has historically run through the Western-dominated International Group of P&I Clubs, the cap assumed sellers had no credible alternative.

The workaround defeats that assumption at the ownership layer. Opaque single-purpose entities acquire aging tankers and flag them under open registries that apply minimal beneficial-ownership scrutiny, producing a fleet structurally outside Western cover from the start. The Kyiv School of Economics (KSE) Institute, which runs a monthly Russian Shadow Fleet Tracker, documents the next phase: the share of shadow tankers flying Russia's own flag, rather than a third-country flag of convenience, rose from 3% to 21% over nine months into early 2026. A September 2026 analysis by the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air (CREA) found the inverse metric — vessels using false foreign flags — falling, from a peak of 101 in October 2025 to 45 by June 2026, and argues this is substitution, not retreat: a genuinely Russian-flagged vessel changes the legal and diplomatic calculus of any interdiction, versus a vessel merely lying about its registry.

Identification is the remaining enforcement lever, and it's degrading too. Tankers increasingly disable or spoof their AIS (Automatic Identification System) transponder — the VHF position broadcast mandated under SOLAS — especially around ship-to-ship transfers used to obscure cargo provenance. Maritime-intelligence firm Windward's Q2 2026 risk report put its tracked "dark fleet" past 2,180 vessels, roughly half under active sanctions, and recorded a nearly fivefold quarter-on-quarter jump in vessels going AIS-dark, with Russian-linked shipping alone logging almost 4,000 transponder blackout events. Rival tracker Kpler has responded by integrating daily Airbus SPOT optical satellite imagery into its AIS feed, specifically to cross-reference vessels through reporting gaps in the Gulf, Black Sea, Baltic, and Red Sea — treating enforcement as a remote-sensing and data-fusion problem now that the identity layer itself is compromised.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: A sanctions regime built entirely on insurance and flags is now an arms race between spoofed ship transponders and the satellites built to catch them.
