November 25, 2026 GOVERNANCE RISK

Nvidia Has Had Exactly One CEO Since It Was Founded in 1993. On November 12, He Resigned With Zero Notice.

For all 33 years of Nvidia's existence, Jensen Huang was its only CEO — the person who personally built the relationships now holding the AI chip supply chain together. On November 12 he resigned, effective immediately, with no permanent successor named. The scandal made headlines. The governance story is bigger.

Key takeaway "Key person risk" is a supply-chain concept usually applied to a single mine or a single factory. This is the first time it has shown up, in full public view, at the top of the company that sits at the center of the entire AI chip supply chain — and the org chart didn't change at all. The relationships did.

The plain version

Nvidia makes the chips that power basically every major AI system on Earth, and for all 33 years of the company's existence, it has had exactly one CEO: Jensen Huang, the guy in the leather jacket who became the public face of the entire AI boom. On November 12, he resigned, effective immediately — no transition period, no retirement tour, just a filing naming the chief financial officer, Colette Kress, as interim CEO while the board searches for someone permanent.

The headlines focused on why. Multiple outlets reported Huang had told staff he'd been unfaithful to his wife, and that U.S. officials suspected the woman involved had ties to Chinese intelligence. Nvidia's board said it had no evidence that company secrets or national-security information had leaked. That's the scandal. The supply-chain story is different, and arguably bigger.

For a decade, Huang wasn't just running Nvidia — he was personally the handshake behind nearly every chokepoint in the AI chip supply chain. He's the one who convinced TSMC to triple its advanced chip-packaging lines years before most people believed the AI boom was real. He's the one who flew to Washington and Beijing, repeatedly, to negotiate which chips Nvidia could legally sell into China. He's the one whose name is on the eye-watering multi-year compute deals with OpenAI, Microsoft, and Oracle. None of that lives in a contract that automatically transfers to a new CEO — it lives in 33 years of relationships one person built. The board insists nothing changes. The honest answer is: nobody knows yet, because this has never happened before.

The expert version

Nvidia filed an 8-K on November 12, 2026, disclosing that Jensen Huang had resigned as president, CEO, and director, effective immediately, after 33 years running the company he co-founded in 1993. CFO Colette Kress was named interim CEO while retaining her finance role, and the board appointed Harvey C. Jones — previously CEO and executive chairman of Synopsys — as the company's first-ever independent board chair. The filing gave no reason for the departure; separate reporting tied it to Huang's admission of an affair and unconfirmed allegations about the other party's intelligence ties, which the board said it found no evidence had compromised company or national-security information. Nvidia shares fell as much as roughly 12–14% intraday that day (reports differ on the exact figure), on top of weeks of AI-capex-bubble jitters already underway.

The governance-risk angle is what matters for supply chains specifically. Nvidia functions today as the central allocator of AI compute capacity: separate TrendForce- and UBS-based estimates reported by Wccftech and DigiTimes put Nvidia's claim on TSMC's CoWoS-L advanced packaging capacity at over 70% in recent years, with 2026 bookings cited anywhere from roughly 678,000 to 850,000 wafers — the two figures don't reconcile, but both show Nvidia's order alone dwarfing AMD's or Broadcom's. On Nvidia's most recent earnings call (November 2025), Kress — then CFO — told investors the company had "visibility" to roughly $500 billion in combined Blackwell-and-Rubin-architecture revenue running through the end of calendar 2026, a figure that depends on Nvidia's roadmap translating into HBM orders from SK Hynix, Samsung, and Micron, and foundry capacity reserved years in advance.

Each of those positions — the TSMC packaging allocation, the China export-license negotiations with Commerce, the multi-hundred-billion-dollar hyperscaler contracts — was built through Huang's direct, personal relationships over a decade, not through replicable institutional process. "Key person risk" is a standard supply-chain category normally applied to sole-source suppliers: a single mine, a single fab. This is the first time it has applied, in full view, to the governance of the company sitting at the center of the chokepoint itself. The board's public position is continuity. What it cannot assert is that relationships inherited by a not-yet-named permanent CEO perform identically to ones personally built over 33 years.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: Nvidia sits at the center of the entire AI chip supply chain, and its board just discovered, in real time, how much of that position was never actually institutional.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: A chokepoint company losing the one person who personally held its supplier, customer, and regulatory relationships together is a live test of whether "key person risk" can be managed at the scale the AI boom now runs on.
