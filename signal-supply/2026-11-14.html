November 14, 2026 SOLDER METALS

Indonesia's Anti-Smuggling Crackdown Cut Tin Exports by as Much as 47% This Year. That Metal Solders Every Chip on Earth to Its Board.

Indonesia supplies roughly a fifth of the world's mined tin and nearly a quarter of its refined exports — and by its own government's estimate, up to 80% of that tin used to come from illegal, unlicensed mines. Jakarta's crackdown on that trade is real, and it's working. It has also helped push tin to a record price, because solder — the tin alloy that bonds every chip to every circuit board, including the ones inside AI servers — doesn't care whether the metal in it was mined legally.

Key takeaway Indonesia's refined tin exports fell roughly 47% year-over-year in January 2026 and were still down about 18% year-over-year in September, as President Prabowo's crackdown on roughly 1,000 illegal mines in Bangka Belitung — intensified by an August 2026 customs probe into the smuggling network — collided with new export-licensing rules. LME tin hit a record near $59,000 a tonne in June 2026, up more than 35% for the year, with the global refined market running an estimated 3% supply deficit. Solder is tin's single largest end use by most industry estimates (around half of global consumption); AI servers are only an estimated 3–4% of total tin demand, but they're nearly all of its recent growth — bidding against a legal supply that just got smaller.

Before the crackdown, Indonesia's tin trade looked healthy from a distance — but by the government's own estimate, up to 80% of that tin was moving through unlicensed mines and smuggling networks that dodged royalties, taxes, and safety rules, then landed in the same global market as the legal metal.

The plain version

Every chip in your phone, laptop, or an AI server's GPU is connected to its circuit board by something almost nobody thinks about: solder. It's a metal alloy — mostly tin — that gets heated until it melts, flows into place around a chip's tiny connection points, and then cools into a solid joint. No solder, no connection, no computer. Nothing else does this job at this scale and price.

Here's the twist: a huge share of the world's tin comes from Indonesia, and a huge share of Indonesia's tin has historically come from illegal mining — small, unlicensed operations digging up tin ore and smuggling it into the legal supply chain, dodging taxes and safety rules along the way. By the Indonesian government's own estimate, as much as 80% of the country's tin has flowed through that shadow system.

Indonesia's president has spent the past year trying to shut it down: ordering roughly 1,000 illegal mines closed, tightening who can even hold an export license, and launching a customs probe into who's been smuggling tin out the back door. It's a legitimate anti-corruption push. But legal and illegal tin end up in the exact same global market, bought by the exact same solder makers. Choke off a big chunk of a country's supply chain overnight, and honest buyers don't get a cleaner alternative — they just get less tin, at a much higher price.

That's roughly what happened: Indonesia's tin exports cratered at points in 2026, and tin itself hit a record price on global markets. The AI boom didn't cause this squeeze, but it's making it worse — every GPU server being built right now needs its own solder joints, and that extra demand is landing on a market that just lost a chunk of its honest supply.

The expert version

Tin's dominant end use is solder — on the order of half of global refined tin consumption by most industry estimates (Fastmarkets puts it near 52%) — because tin-based alloys (commonly SAC305: roughly 96.5% tin, 3% silver, 0.5% copper, the lead-free standard under RoHS) form the metallurgical joint between a chip's bond pads or ball-grid-array bumps and its substrate or PCB. That joint must survive repeated thermal cycling without fatiguing, which rules out most cheaper substitutes at scale.

Indonesia is the structural supply node. The IEA's Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026 puts Indonesia at roughly 21% of global tin mine production (second to China's ~24%), and Project Blue data shows Indonesia supplied about 23% of global unwrought-tin exports in 2025 — the largest single-country share. Indonesian officials and independent reporting have long estimated that up to ~80% of the country's historical output came from unlicensed, artisanal operations in Bangka Belitung, smuggled into the legal export chain through falsified documentation.

President Prabowo ordered a crackdown starting September 2025 (targeting roughly 1,000 illegal mines), tightened export licensing (a new "PE Industrial Tin" permit regime took effect by April 2026), and in August 2026 directed a customs probe into the smuggling network itself. The measured effect on legal trade has been severe: Indonesia's refined tin exports fell roughly 47% year-over-year in January 2026, bottomed near 2,255 metric tons in April under the new licensing rules, and were still down about 18% year-over-year in September. Industry data put the global refined market in a supply deficit of roughly 3% through mid-2026.

Price responded accordingly: LME tin hit a record near $59,000/tonne in June 2026, up more than 35% year-to-date, with forecasters revising full-year average estimates upward toward $51,500/tonne. AI servers are not the structural driver — one estimate puts AI at only 3–4% of total tin demand — but they are the only source of incremental demand growth, so new AI buildouts are bidding against an already-tightened legal supply for a material with no economical substitute in interconnect.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: an anti-corruption crackdown most tech buyers have never heard of is quietly raising the cost of every soldered joint on Earth — including the ones inside every AI server being built right now.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: tin has no economical substitute in interconnect, so a single country's enforcement policy on artisanal mining can move global electronics BOM costs independent of anything happening in chips, wafers, or packaging.
