October 30, 2026 FREIGHT RATES

Global Ocean Freight Rates Have Jumped 130% Since February. Not One Ship Sank — They're Just Taking a 3,000-Mile Detour.

Drewry's benchmark container index has been sitting near $4,400–$4,600 a box through October, more than double where it was in February. No ship was lost and no factory burned down. Hundreds of vessels are simply sailing around Africa instead of through the Suez Canal — and that detour alone behaves like shrinking the world's merchant fleet.

Key takeaway Since Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping escalated, most big container lines have rerouted Asia–Europe strings around the Cape of Good Hope instead of through Suez. On the reference Shenzhen–Rotterdam run, that swaps a roughly 10,000-nautical-mile, 31-day voyage for a roughly 13,000-mile, 41-day one — UNCTAD's figures. Because every ship takes proportionally longer per round trip, the same vessels complete fewer trips a year; analysts at UBS estimated this cuts effective Asia–Europe capacity by about a quarter. Trade trackers still counted roughly 250–300 ships on the Cape routing in October 2026. Layer renewed Middle East tensions on top, and Jefferies says global container rates are up about 130% since a February 2026 baseline — which is exactly what Drewry's index has been pricing in all month.

The plain version

Picture a fleet of delivery trucks that normally take the highway between two warehouses. The highway becomes dangerous, so every truck starts taking a backroad detour that's about 30% longer. Nobody's truck gets stolen or breaks down — but because every round trip now takes longer, the fleet can make fewer deliveries per week. That's roughly what's happening right now to the ships carrying nearly everything in a store near you, from phone chargers to flat-screen TVs to the specialized tools that make chips.

Since Houthi militants in Yemen started attacking ships in the Red Sea, most big container lines stopped sailing through the Suez Canal, the famous Egyptian shortcut between Asia and Europe. Instead, they go the old-fashioned way: around the southern tip of Africa, past the Cape of Good Hope. A trip from southern China to Rotterdam that used to cover about 10,000 nautical miles and take roughly a month now covers around 13,000 miles and takes about 41 days — ten extra days, one way.

Multiply that across hundreds of ships and you get a strange effect: the world's shipping fleet hasn't lost a single vessel, but it's doing meaningfully less work, because every ship is stuck on a longer loop. Analysts estimated that when the detour was near-universal, it ate up roughly a quarter of the capacity on the Asia–Europe route. That's a big part of why the price to ship a standard 40-foot container has climbed toward $4,400–$4,600 recently — and why, per the bank Jefferies, global freight rates are up about 130% since February 2026. Somebody pays that bill eventually; it's usually baked quietly into the price of whatever's inside the box.

The expert version

The relevant distinction is nominal versus effective capacity. Nominal capacity is how much cargo (in TEU, twenty-foot-equivalent units) the global container fleet could carry if every ship sailed the shortest route. Effective capacity is what the fleet actually delivers per unit time, which depends on round-trip duration: a vessel that takes longer per loop completes fewer loops per year, so it delivers fewer TEU-miles of throughput even though its hull hasn't changed.

Since Houthi attacks escalated in the Red Sea and Bab-el-Mandeb strait, most major container lines rerouted Asia–Europe strings around the Cape of Good Hope rather than through Suez. UNCTAD's reference comparison (Shenzhen–Rotterdam) puts the Suez routing at roughly 10,000 nautical miles and 31 days, versus roughly 13,000 nautical miles and 41 days via the Cape — about 30% more distance and 32% more transit time for the identical origin-destination pair. When diversions were near-total in 2023–2024, UBS analysts estimated this cut effective Asia–Europe carrying capacity by about 25%, even as the nominal global fleet kept growing through scheduled newbuild deliveries.

As of October 2026 the diversion is partial but still substantial: trade trackers cited by Ship Universe put roughly 250–300 vessels still routing via the Cape, down from an estimated 380 ships and 5.4 million TEU earlier in the year, as some alliances test a partial return to Suez. That ongoing uncertainty, layered on renewed regional tensions, feeds directly into pricing: Drewry's World Container Index, an eight-route composite, held in the $4,350–$4,590-per-40-foot-container range through October 2026 (Shanghai–Rotterdam near $3,686 and Shanghai–Genoa near $4,151 on October 29), and Jefferies estimated on October 19 that global container rates have risen about 130% since a February 2026 baseline of roughly $2,000–$2,500. Current levels remain well below 2021's pandemic-era extremes, when spot rates on some lanes briefly topped five figures per box — this is a capacity-reallocation repricing, not an absolute vessel shortage.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: Those extra African miles are a quiet tax on everything shipped from Asia to Europe — including a lot of tech hardware — and nobody had to lose a single ship for it to show up in the price.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: The Cape detour shows how geography, not hull count, sets effective ocean-freight capacity — a mechanism that will resurface any time a chokepoint like Suez, Panama, or Hormuz gets contested, regardless of how big the nominal fleet is.
