November 6, 2026 CHIP DESIGN SOFTWARE

Three Companies License the Software That Designs Every Advanced Chip. In 2025, Washington Switched China's Off — With a Memo, Not a Ship.

Every chokepoint in this newsletter so far has been a thing you could point to: a machine, a mineral, a port. This one is different. It's a login screen. Three companies' software sits between every chip idea on Earth and an actual, working transistor — and for six weeks in 2025, one government notice decided whether China's chip engineers could open it.

Key takeaway Synopsys, Cadence, and Siemens EDA together hold roughly three-quarters of the global market for electronic design automation (EDA) software — the tools that turn a chip idea into a manufacturable layout. No advanced chip, anywhere, reaches a fab without passing through at least one of their products. In May 2025, the U.S. Commerce Department told all three they'd need a government license to keep selling and supporting that software for customers in China. Synopsys pulled its full-year guidance; both firms disclosed roughly a tenth of their revenue tied to China. Roughly six weeks later, the restriction was quietly rescinded as part of a broader trade truce in which China eased its own rare-earth export curbs. No tanker changed course. No factory opened or closed. The entire exchange happened inside paperwork.

The plain version

Before any advanced chip — the one in your phone, your car, Nvidia's latest AI accelerator — becomes a physical object, someone has to design it using specialized software. This software, called EDA (electronic design automation), is what lets engineers describe how billions of microscopic switches should connect, then checks that the resulting blueprint will actually work and can actually be manufactured. It's less like a drafting tool and more like the compiler, debugger, and building inspector for a chip, rolled into one.

Here's the strange part: almost this entire industry runs through three companies. Synopsys and Cadence, both American, and Siemens EDA, part of the German industrial giant, together control roughly three-quarters of this software market. There is effectively no cutting-edge chip on the planet that didn't pass through at least one of their products on its way from idea to factory.

In May 2025, the U.S. government used that fact as leverage. It told the three companies they needed a government license to keep selling and supporting their design software for Chinese customers — treating a sale of software the same way it treats a sale of a missile component. All three complied. Synopsys later said the disruption to its China business, combined with a rough quarter, helped drive its stock to its worst single-day drop in decades.

Then, about six weeks later, Washington reversed course, as part of a broader trade deal in which China eased its own restrictions on exporting rare-earth minerals. Nobody moved a ship. Nobody opened or closed a factory. The entire episode happened and ended inside a software license agreement — which is exactly what makes it worth noticing: this chokepoint has no customs line, no port, and no travel time. It can be switched off, or back on, by a memo.

The expert version

Electronic design automation covers the software toolchain a chip passes through on its way from RTL (register-transfer level) description to a fab-ready GDSII file: functional simulation and formal verification, logic synthesis, physical place-and-route, parasitic extraction, and physical signoff — design-rule checking (DRC) and layout-versus-schematic (LVS) verification against a specific process node's manufacturing rules. Market trackers put combined share for the "Big Three" — Synopsys (~31%), Cadence (~30%), and Siemens EDA (~13%) — at roughly three-quarters of a global EDA market estimated at $15–18 billion a year (estimates vary by methodology and whether adjacent IP/Ansys-style simulation revenue is included). Within that, concentration is uneven by sub-step: Siemens' Calibre alone is widely cited as holding more than 70% of the physical-signoff/DRC market, a chokepoint inside the chokepoint.

On May 29–30, 2025, Synopsys, Cadence, and Siemens EDA each disclosed receiving informal notice from the Bureau of Industry and Security that new export-control rules required a license to sell or support EDA software for customers in China, citing risk of diversion to Chinese military end use. Synopsys suspended full-year guidance, citing roughly 10% of its ~$1.6 billion quarterly revenue tied to Chinese customers; it later reported a 34% single-day stock decline — its worst in over three decades — alongside an 8% drop in its Design IP segment that CEO Sassine Ghazi attributed in part to deals disrupted by the restriction. Cadence disclosed roughly 12% of prior-year revenue tied to China and confirmed its own license requirement. BIS rescinded the EDA-specific restriction in early July 2025, inside six weeks of imposing it, as part of a wider bilateral trade framework under which China eased its own April 2025 rare-earth export controls.

China's policy response has centered on Empyrean Technology (Huada Jiutian), the largest domestic EDA vendor, historically holding only a single-digit share of even China's own domestic EDA spend as foreign tools remained dominant inside China itself. Through 2026, Chinese industry reporting (TrendForce, citing government and company disclosures) describes the number of EDA tool categories in "large-scale commercial use" domestically rising from 17 to 26, alongside Empyrean's push into AI-assisted layout/verification and full-flow memory-design tools aligned with Huawei's published chip-design methodology. That is real progress toward substitution, not yet parity: covering more tool categories is not the same as covering the full, leading-edge flow the Big Three still dominate end to end.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: Every other chokepoint in this newsletter is a thing you can point to — a machine, a mineral, a ship. This one is pure permission, and it's revocable by memo, with no port or shipping lane in between.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: EDA sits structurally upstream of every other chip chokepoint — fabs, lithography, advanced packaging — and because it's delivered as a license rather than a shipment, it's the fastest-switching control the U.S. currently holds over China's leading-edge chip design capacity.
