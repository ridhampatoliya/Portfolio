October 28, 2026 MARITIME NAVIGATION

GPS Jamming Knocked Out Navigation on More Than 1,100 Ships in a Single Day. Spoofing Is the Quieter Failure Mode — It Doesn't Tell the Crew Anything Is Wrong.

Every ship crossing an ocean trusts a satellite signal faint enough to drown out with cheap hardware. One way to attack it makes the screen go blank. The other way is worse: it makes the screen lie, confidently, with no alarm at all.

Key takeaway On February 28, 2026, electronic interference tied to the Israel-Iran conflict disrupted navigation on more than 1,100 commercial ships in a single day in and around the Strait of Hormuz — the waterway carrying roughly 20% of the world's oil and gas exports. Lloyd's List Intelligence logged over 1,700 separate GPS interference events on more than 650 vessels in the days that followed. Jamming, the cruder attack, at least tells a crew something's wrong. Spoofing doesn't — it hands the ship a false position that looks every bit as confident as a real one.

The plain version

Every big ship, plane, and container crossing an ocean navigates by GPS: four or more satellites broadcasting a timing signal so faint that a receiver has to be tuned carefully to hear it at all. That faintness is also GPS's biggest weakness, because it's cheap to drown out or fake.

There are two very different ways to attack it. Jamming is the blunt version: flood the frequency with noise until a ship's receiver can't hear the real satellites anymore. The result is obvious — the screen goes blank, an alarm sounds, and the crew knows immediately something's wrong. That's effectively what happened to more than 1,100 commercial vessels in the Gulf on a single day in late February 2026, after fighting between Israel and Iran spilled into the Strait of Hormuz, the waterway that carries roughly a fifth of the world's oil and gas exports. Lloyd's List Intelligence logged over 1,700 separate interference events on more than 650 ships in the days that followed.

Spoofing is the sneakier version, and arguably the more dangerous one. Instead of jamming the signal, an attacker broadcasts a fake one that's stronger and looks completely legitimate — so the receiver locks onto it and reports a confident, consistent position. The catch: it's the wrong position. The crew sees nothing unusual on screen; the ship just quietly drifts off its real course, sometimes toward rocks, shoals, or another ship's path. The Baltic and Black Sea have both logged rising numbers of these incidents for years, mostly attributed to Russia, though no government has admitted responsibility.

The fix nobody's fully built yet: a backup navigation signal that's hard to fake, broadcast from the ground instead of space.

The expert version

GPS position-fixing works by trilateration: a receiver measures the time delay of signals from at least four satellites, each broadcasting a precise timestamp, and solves for the one location consistent with all four ranges. The signal reaching the surface is extraordinarily weak, and civilian GPS is broadcast unencrypted on a well-documented frequency and format — which makes it straightforward to overpower or imitate with inexpensive hardware.

Jamming floods that frequency with noise until the receiver can no longer achieve carrier lock on any satellite, producing a hard failure: no position, no velocity, an explicit fault indication. Spoofing instead transmits a counterfeit signal set, synchronized and stronger than the genuine one, that the receiver's tracking loop accepts as real; the receiver then computes and reports a false position with no fault flag at all, because from the receiver's perspective nothing is wrong. That asymmetry — a jammed receiver screams, a spoofed one lies calmly — is why maritime security researchers treat spoofing as the more operationally dangerous failure mode.

The scale is no longer theoretical. Windward's tracking identified disrupted navigation on more than 1,100 commercial vessels across UAE, Qatari, Omani, and Iranian waters on February 28, 2026, as the Israel-Iran conflict escalated; Lloyd's List Intelligence separately logged 1,735 discrete GPS interference events affecting 655 vessels through March 3. The Strait of Hormuz those ships were transiting carries roughly 20% of global oil and gas trade by volume. The Baltic shows a parallel, slower-moving pattern: a joint Baltic-state submission to ICAO attributed disruption affecting roughly 123,000 flights between January and April 2025 to Russia-linked sources, and Danish authorities recorded record interference levels near Bornholm in August 2026, including one disruption that persisted for a full day versus a typical sub-20-minute episode.

The proposed hedge is eLoran — a terrestrial, high-power radio signal reported by its backers to be roughly 1.3 million times stronger than GPS and far harder to jam — but adoption is fragmented: South Korea and Russia are building national systems, the U.S. Coast Guard's program lost funding years ago, and Europe is split, with France and Germany leaning on Galileo instead.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: Ships, planes, and the ports that depend on them run on a navigation signal that's cheap to fake, and the scariest failure mode doesn't even trigger an alarm.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: Position, navigation, and timing underpin shipping schedules, port operations, and insurance pricing across global trade — and the industry's primary PNT source still has no authenticated, universally adopted backup.
