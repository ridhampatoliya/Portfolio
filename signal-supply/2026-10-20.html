October 20, 2026 CHIP PACKAGING

Taiwan and China Build Roughly 70% of the World's Packaged Chips. India Just Opened Three Plants to Compete — None of Which Can Fabricate One.

This year India switched on its first "semiconductor" plants, and a lot of the coverage made it sound like India had joined the chipmaking club. It hasn't — not the way you'd think. Every plant that opened is an OSAT facility: it assembles, tests and packages chips that were fabricated somewhere else. That's not a knock on the achievement. It's a real, if partial, bid to break into a back-end chokepoint Taiwan and China have quietly owned for decades.

Key takeaway India's new chip plants are Outsourced Semiconductor Assembly and Test (OSAT) facilities, not wafer fabs. Micron's Sanand, Gujarat ATMP plant (roughly $2.75B, with the Indian government covering up to 50% of capex under the India Semiconductor Mission) began operations February 28, 2026; Kaynes Semicon's Sanand OSAT followed on March 31; CG Power's Semi OSAT targets commercial operations later this year; HCL–Foxconn broke ground near Delhi in February. None of them etch a transistor. Tata Electronics is separately building India's first actual wafer fab, at Dholera, Gujarat — not expected before 2028. Globally, Taiwan's ASE alone holds roughly 44.6% of the OSAT market (TrendForce, 2024), with Taiwan and China combined commonly put above 70%.

The plain version

This year, India opened its first "semiconductor" plants, and the headlines made it sound like India had joined the chipmaking club alongside Taiwan, South Korea and the US. Micron switched on a $2.75 billion facility in Sanand, Gujarat, in February. Two more OSAT plants opened nearby within weeks. A fourth broke ground outside Delhi. Here's the part most of those headlines buried: not one of these plants can turn a blank disc of silicon into a working chip.

That step — carving billions of microscopic transistors onto a silicon wafer using lithography machines that cost more than a jumbo jet — is called wafer fabrication, or simply "the fab." It still happens almost nowhere outside Taiwan, South Korea, the US and Japan. What India just built is everything that happens after the fab: slicing the finished wafer into individual chips, testing them, wiring each one into its protective casing, and testing it again before it ships. In the industry, this back-end work has a name — OSAT, for Outsourced Semiconductor Assembly and Test.

It's less glamorous than "making chips from scratch," but it's genuinely valuable, and it's genuinely scarce: Taiwan and China together are estimated to handle more than 70% of the world's chip packaging today, with Taiwan's ASE alone commanding nearly 45% of the global OSAT market. Every phone, laptop and AI server in the world passes through that narrow pipeline. By building packaging plants instead of waiting on a fab, India picked the faster, cheaper way to grab a real piece of a real chokepoint. Tata is separately attempting the harder, far more expensive step — an actual wafer fab — but it won't open before 2028.

The expert version

The semiconductor value chain splits cleanly into "front-end" and "back-end." Front-end is wafer fabrication: photolithography, ion implantation (doping), etching and deposition, building up transistor structures on a silicon wafer across hundreds of process steps — the domain of TSMC, Samsung, Intel and GlobalFoundries, requiring leading-edge fabs that now cost upward of $15–20 billion each. Back-end is ATMP/OSAT (Assembly, Test, Marking, Packaging / Outsourced Semiconductor Assembly and Test): wafer probe/sort, dicing, die attach, wire-bond or flip-chip interconnect, encapsulation, and final electrical test — far less capital-intensive per unit of output, but still precision clean-room work at scale.

India's 2026 entrants are squarely back-end. Micron's Sanand, Gujarat ATMP facility — roughly $2.75 billion, with the Indian government covering up to 50% of capex under the India Semiconductor Mission (ISM) — began operations February 28, 2026, importing wafers Micron fabricates elsewhere (the US, Japan, Singapore, Taiwan) and performing assembly, test, marking and packaging locally. Kaynes Semicon's Sanand OSAT followed on March 31; CG Power's Semi OSAT at the same hub targets commercial operations later this year; HCL–Foxconn broke ground on a plant at Jewar, Uttar Pradesh in February. Tata Electronics is running two separate, much larger tracks: a ₹27,000 crore (~$3.2B) OSAT/ATMP facility at Morigaon, Assam, and a ₹91,000+ crore (~$11B) wafer fab at Dholera, Gujarat — India's first attempt at actual front-end fabrication, targeting 2028, with reported mobilization of over 7,000 personnel. (Published ISM 2.0 capex-support tiers vary by source: some put ATMP/OSAT support as high as 50%, others describe a tiered 25–40% structure by packaging complexity; public reporting doesn't resolve the discrepancy as of this writing.)

The competitive landscape India is entering is concentrated. TrendForce data puts Taiwan's ASE at roughly 44.6% of the global OSAT market in 2024, with Amkor second (15.2%), and China's JCET (12%) and Tongfu Microelectronics (8%) close behind — five Chinese firms now sit in the global top 10, per 2025 rankings. Taiwan and China combined are commonly cited above 70% of world OSAT capacity, against a global OSAT market sized around $46.5–49.6 billion in 2026. India starts from near-zero share. The strategic logic mirrors the "fab diversification" push seen in chips generally: back-end concentration in Taiwan and China is as real a single point of failure as front-end concentration in Taiwan alone — and OSAT capacity, while far cheaper to build than a fab, is the stage India can actually move the needle on today.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: Three new Indian plants won't make India a chipmaking power overnight, but they do chip away at the 70%-plus share Taiwan and China hold over the unglamorous back-end step every chip needs before it ships.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: OSAT/back-end packaging carries the same Taiwan-and-China concentration risk as front-end fabrication — and it's the stage India can credibly contest years before any Indian wafer fab ships a working chip.
