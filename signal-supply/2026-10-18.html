October 18, 2026 SHIPBUILDING

China Builds 72% of the World's New Ships. The U.S. Tariff Meant to Answer That Comes Back in 22 Days.

A U.S. tariff on Chinese-built and Chinese-owned ships was supposed to be running by now, stepping up every April through 2028. Instead, Washington and Beijing paused it a year ago as part of a trade truce — and that pause is scheduled to end at 11:59 p.m. Eastern on November 9. Nobody has made the promised extension official yet.

Key takeaway Under a Section 301 "shipbuilding" action, the U.S. set per-net-ton port fees on Chinese-owned/operated vessels ($50, stepping to $80 on April 17, 2026, then $110 in 2027, $140 in 2028) and on Chinese-built vessels under other flags ($18 stepping to $23, $28, $33). Days before the October 14, 2025 start date, Trump and Xi met in Busan and both sides suspended their fees for one year, effective November 10, 2025. On September 23, 2026, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said that truce would extend to January 10, 2027 — but as of the most recent reporting, the U.S. Trade Representative had not yet published the Federal Register notice that would make it legally binding. Absent that notice, the suspension lapses at 11:59 p.m. ET on November 9.

The plain version

Look at almost any big ship in the water right now — the container ships, the oil tankers, the car carriers — and there's a good chance a Chinese shipyard built it. By some counts, Chinese yards took roughly 7 to 8 out of every 10 new ship orders placed worldwide this year. The United States, which built a huge share of the world's ships during World War II, now builds a rounding error: about three one-hundredths of one percent of the world's new ship tonnage in each of the last two years, ranking 19th globally, with American-built ships costing four to six times more than ones built abroad.

Washington treats this as more than an industrial embarrassment. A navy that has to fight a long war needs a healthy civilian shipbuilding and repair industry behind it — extra yards, extra workers, extra capacity to call on. The U.S. barely has that anymore. So in 2025, trade officials rolled out a tariff-like fee: any ship that's built in China, or owned or operated by a Chinese company, would pay a charge — by the ton — every time it called at a U.S. port, with the fee getting bigger each year through 2028. For an average container ship, that can mean millions of dollars added to a single voyage.

Then, right before it was due to start, the U.S. and China struck a one-year truce and froze the whole thing — the U.S. fee and China's matching retaliation fee on American ships, both. Why? Because nearly everything the world ships, including the machinery that builds AI data centers and chip factories, moves on vessels with some Chinese fingerprint on them. That truce is set to run out on November 9. A top U.S. official said in September that it would be pushed back two more months. Nobody has made that official yet.

The expert version

The fee originates in USTR's Section 301 investigation into China's targeting of the maritime, logistics, and shipbuilding sectors, which produced two fee annexes assessed per net ton on arrival at a U.S. port, capped at five chargeable port calls per vessel per year. Annex I covers vessels owned or operated by a Chinese entity: $50/net ton effective October 14, 2025, stepping to $80 on April 17, 2026, $110 on April 17, 2027, and $140 on April 17, 2028. Annex II covers vessels built in China but owned/operated by non-Chinese carriers: the operator pays the greater of a per-net-ton fee ($18 → $23 → $28 → $33 on the same step dates) or a per-container charge, with carve-outs for empty/ballast calls, small and short-sea vessels, certain U.S.-flagged ships, and some long-term-chartered LNG/LPG carriers ordered before April 2025.

The policy targets a measured asymmetry. By UNCTAD-sourced tallies, the U.S. delivered roughly 0.03–0.04% of world gross tonnage in 2024 and 2025 combined, ranking 19th globally and averaging about 0.24% over the past decade on a declining trend; domestically built merchant ships are estimated to cost four to six times as much as foreign-built equivalents. China, meanwhile, took roughly 70–85% of 2026's new ship orders depending on the metric: Clarksons puts China at 72% of H1 2026 orders by compensated gross tonnage (versus South Korea's 19%), while China's own Ministry of Industry and Information Technology reports an 82.3% share of H1 new orders by deadweight tonnage. The spread between those two numbers reflects what each metric weights — DWT counts raw cargo capacity, while CGT adjusts for a ship type's build complexity — not a factual disagreement.

Days before the fee's scheduled start, Presidents Trump and Xi met in Busan and agreed to a broader trade truce: effective November 10, 2025, the U.S. suspended its Section 301 port fees for one year, China reciprocally suspended its own retaliatory port fees on U.S.-linked vessels, and both sides paused separately threatened 100% tariffs on ship-to-shore cranes and container chassis. On September 23, 2026, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the two governments had agreed to extend that truce to January 10, 2027. As of the most recent reporting available, USTR had not yet published a Federal Register notice formalizing an extension specific to the port-fee suspension, which under the existing notice lapses at 11:59 p.m. ET on November 9, 2026 — after which the next qualifying port call would again owe a fee, at whichever rate the written schedule specifies for that date.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: Nearly every chip, server and EV battery crosses an ocean on a ship this fee would tax — and nobody has made the extension official yet.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: Semiconductor capital equipment, memory modules, finished servers and EV battery cells move almost entirely by containership on vessels overwhelmingly built or operated out of China, so a lapsed suspension would land as an unplanned, multimillion-dollar-per-call cost layered on top of existing chip and goods tariffs — with carriers and shippers currently unable to confirm which rate, if any, would even apply.
