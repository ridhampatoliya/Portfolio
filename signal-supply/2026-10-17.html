October 17, 2026 FLASH MEMORY

Kioxia Sold Out This Year's Entire NAND Flash Supply in January. Nine Months Later, Your SSD Still Isn't Getting Cheaper.

No factory burned down. No country blocked an export. A Kioxia executive just said, out loud, that the whole year's flash-memory output was already spoken for — and that the drought would likely run into 2027. The reason: AI servers want the same chips your laptop's SSD is made of, and they're willing to pay more to get served first.

Key takeaway On January 22, 2026, Kioxia memory-business head Shunsuke Nakato told reporters at a Seoul event that the company's entire 2026 NAND production was already sold, that the era of the cheap 1TB SSD (around 7,000 yen, roughly $45) was over, and that the shortage would likely persist into 2027. Weeks later, SanDisk quietly raised prices across its own online store by as much as 2.8x — its flagship WD Black SN8100 8TB rose from a $999.99 MSRP to $2,572.99. By April, Samsung and Kingston had both pushed through further double-digit SSD price hikes. Industry trackers now put the cheapest 1TB SATA SSD at roughly $73 — more than 50% above its 2023 low. Phison CEO Khein-Seng Pua had already flagged the root cause in November 2025: spot prices for TLC 1-terabit NAND dies had more than doubled in four months, from $4.80 to $10.70, and new NAND fab capacity wouldn't fully come online until 2027 — with the squeeze potentially lasting up to a decade.

The plain version

Picture a popcorn machine that makes a fixed amount of popcorn every hour, no matter how hungry the room gets. For years, that machine fed two groups fairly evenly: companies building laptops and USB drives, and companies building servers. Then AI showed up starving. AI data centers need the same flash-memory chips your SSD uses — not just for giant GPU clusters, but because AI servers are increasingly swapping old-fashioned spinning hard drives for SSDs to store and quickly serve the mountains of data these systems chew through. Those AI buyers sign contracts years ahead and pay whatever it takes to stand at the front of the line.

The amount of flash memory the world's factories can make barely changes from year to year. Building one of these factories, and getting its fantastically fiddly manufacturing process reliable — it stacks hundreds of microscopic memory cells on top of each other, one wafer-thin layer at a time — takes two to three years minimum, not months. So when AI buyers crowd the popcorn machine, there's less left for everyone else, cheap laptop SSD included.

That's exactly what a Kioxia executive admitted in January: the company's entire year of flash output was already sold, and the drought would likely run into 2027. Weeks later, SanDisk quietly hiked prices on some of its own store's SSDs by as much as 2.8 times. By spring, Samsung and Kingston had both raised prices again. The cheapest basic 1-terabyte SSD now costs around $73 — more than 50% above its cheapest point in 2023. There's no fire, no ban, no villain. Just a fixed pipe, and a much thirstier customer standing at the front of it.

The expert version

NAND flash stores data as trapped charge in floating-gate or charge-trap transistor cells; modern "3D NAND" stacks these cells vertically rather than shrinking them laterally. Commercial parts passed 176 layers years ago, and 2026 production clusters around the low-to-mid 200s to low 300s of layers depending on vendor, with Samsung and SK hynix both targeting roughly 400-layer generations this year. Each additional layer demands a correspondingly deeper, more precisely controlled high-aspect-ratio etch and fill step; yield and cycle time both get harder to hold as the stack gets taller, so bit output doesn't scale cleanly with added capex the way an extra logic-fab line can. A genuinely new NAND fab — not a process tweak — takes roughly two to three years from groundbreaking to volume shipments, which is why Phison CEO Khein-Seng Pua told reporters in November 2025 that new NAND lines wouldn't come fully online until 2027, and warned the shortage could persist for up to a decade.

Demand moved faster than supply can. AI infrastructure build-outs are shifting storage architecture away from spinning hard drives toward NAND-based SSDs for both training-data caching and inference-serving pipelines, and hyperscalers negotiate multi-year offtake agreements that let them jump the allocation queue ahead of spot-market consumer buyers. Pua cited spot TLC (triple-level-cell) 1-terabit NAND die prices rising from $4.80 in July 2025 to $10.70 by November — roughly a 123% increase in four months. That spot-price shock worked its way downstream: Kioxia's Shunsuke Nakato said in January that the company's full-year 2026 output was contractually committed; SanDisk raised its direct-store SSD prices by as much as 2.8x in February, with its WD Black SN8100 8TB rising from a $999.99 MSRP to $2,572.99 on SanDisk's own site; and Samsung and Kingston both pushed through further double-digit consumer SSD price increases by April. Several outlets now track the cheapest 1TB SATA SSDs at roughly $73, versus lows near $40–48 in 2023 — a persistent, structural squeeze rather than a one-quarter spike.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: AI's hunger for flash storage is now setting the price of your laptop's SSD — and the fix, a new fab, takes years, not quarters.

Why it matters for tech + supply chain: NAND capacity is inelastic on any timescale shorter than a fab build, so AI offtake contracts are now the marginal buyer setting consumer SSD prices — a dynamic with no near-term exit, since the earliest meaningfully new capacity isn't expected before 2027.
